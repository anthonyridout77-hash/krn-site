If you eat vegan and you’ve just found out you’re pregnant (or you’re planning to be), you might be wondering if your way of eating can carry you both. I guess the key thing is this: a well-planned vegan diet can meet your needs and your baby’s in pregnancy. The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, the American dietitians’ professional body, considers it appropriate for every life stage. The word doing the heavy lifting there is “planned”. A handful of nutrients need a bit more intention, and a couple will need a supplement. Let’s walk through them.

## What nutrients need extra attention on a vegan pregnancy diet?

The nutrients that need deliberate planning are vitamin B12, iron, iodine, calcium, vitamin D, omega-3 fats (DHA and EPA), protein and folate. A carefully thought-out vegan diet can contain all the essential nutrients, but some micronutrients need special attention and may call for supplements, according to a 2023 review published in Cureus.

Planning matters because research published in Nutrients in 2024 found protein and several micronutrient intakes were significantly lower among vegan pregnant women than omnivorous women. That doesn’t mean a vegan pregnancy diet falls short. It means you can’t assume your usual pattern will stretch to cover the extra demands of pregnancy without a few simple little switches.

Queensland Health publishes antenatal guidance for vegetarian and vegan pregnancies, and the Tasmanian Department of Health has an eating well for pregnancy fact sheet for vegan women. Both treat a vegan pregnancy as a valid path, with targeted food choices and the right supplements.

## How much vitamin B12 do you need in pregnancy, and why does it matter?

Your vitamin B12 requirement rises to 2.6 micrograms a day in pregnancy, up from 2.4 micrograms, under the NHMRC Nutrient Reference Values for Australia and New Zealand. B12 matters for your baby’s brain and nervous system development.

Plant foods don’t reliably provide active B12, and fortified foods alone usually won’t get you to the pregnancy target. So on a vegan diet a B12 supplement isn’t optional, it’s part of the plan from preconception right through to breastfeeding. The dose and form can be tailored to you, which is something to sort out with your GP, obstetrician or midwife and your dietitian.

## How do you meet iron and folate needs without animal foods?

Iron needs rise from 18 milligrams to 27 milligrams a day in pregnancy, according to the Nutrient Reference Values. Plant iron (non-haem iron) is absorbed less readily than iron from animal foods, so pair iron-rich foods with vitamin C at the same meal. Think lentils with capsicum, tofu with broccoli, or a bean salad with a squeeze of lemon.

Good plant iron sources include lentils, chickpeas and other legumes, tofu and tempeh, nuts and seeds, wholegrains and leafy greens. Aim to include legumes or another iron-rich alternative every day. I like to think of your iron stores like a reserve tank: pregnancy draws on it, so we build your tank up early rather than playing catch-up.

Folate is the other piece of this picture. Australian guidance recommends a folic acid supplement of at least 400 micrograms a day, started at least one month before conception and continued through the first three months of pregnancy. Your GP may suggest a higher dose in some situations.

## What about calcium, iodine, vitamin D and omega-3 on a vegan diet?

Calcium needs stay at 1,000 milligrams a day in pregnancy for adult women. Vegan sources still need to be deliberate. Calcium-fortified plant milks, tofu set with calcium, tahini and calcium-fortified yoghurt alternatives can all add up across the day.

Iodine supports your baby’s brain development, and the NHMRC recommends a 150 microgram iodine supplement for women who are pregnant, breastfeeding or planning a pregnancy. Iodised salt in cooking and bread made with iodised salt help too, but they won’t cover pregnancy needs on their own.

Vitamin D needs depend on your sun exposure, skin tone and the season, so this is one to check with a blood test and discuss with your maternity team. Many pregnancy multivitamins include vitamin D.

For omega-3, the Adequate Intake for ALA in pregnancy is 1.0 gram a day under the Nutrient Reference Values, from foods like walnuts, chia, linseed and canola oil. ALA converts to DHA only in small amounts, so an algae-based DHA supplement is worth talking through with your dietitian, as it gives you a direct vegan source of the long-chain omega-3 fats.

## Can a vegan diet lower your risk of gestational diabetes?

Vegan pregnancies have been associated with lower rates of gestational diabetes and high blood pressure, according to the 2023 Cureus review. This evidence is observational, so it shows association rather than cause and effect, but the pattern is consistent.

Fibre seems to play a role. In the Nurses’ Health Study II, each 10 gram increase in daily cereal fibre before pregnancy was associated with a 26 percent lower risk of gestational diabetes (Zhang and colleagues, Diabetes Care, 2006). Wholegrains, legumes and half a plate of veg are your friends here, and fibre sweeps everything through nicely too.

## How much extra should you eat, and what about protein?

Energy needs barely change in the first trimester. The Nutrient Reference Values suggest around an extra 1,400 kilojoules a day in the second trimester and around 1,900 kilojoules a day in the third. That’s roughly a pair of snacks, not eating for two.

Protein needs rise to around 60 grams a day in pregnancy, up from about 46 grams. On a vegan diet that means being intentional about tofu, tempeh, legumes, soy milk, nuts and seeds at most meals and snacks. Pairing protein, carbohydrate and fibre together also keeps your energy steadier between meals.

The 2023 Cureus review reports mixed findings on birth weight in vegan pregnancies, with some studies showing lower birth weights and others no meaningful difference. Regular check-ins with your maternity team are the practical answer, so growth and nutrient levels can be tracked as you go.

## Key Takeaways

* A well-planned vegan diet can meet your needs in pregnancy, and planning is the key word.
* Vitamin B12 needs rise to 2.6 micrograms a day in pregnancy, and a supplement is essential if you eat no animal foods.
* Iron needs rise from 18 to 27 milligrams a day, and pairing plant iron with vitamin C at the same meal helps absorption.
* Folic acid (at least 400 micrograms) and iodine (150 micrograms) supplements are recommended for all women planning or in pregnancy.
* Calcium needs stay at 1,000 milligrams a day, but vegan sources need to be planned deliberately.
* Vegan pregnancies have been associated with lower rates of gestational diabetes, and higher fibre intake is linked to lower risk.
* Protein needs rise to around 60 grams a day, so include a plant protein at most meals and snacks.
