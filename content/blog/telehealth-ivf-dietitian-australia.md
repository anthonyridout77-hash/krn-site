<h2 id="can-you-actually-see-an-ivf-dietitian-by-telehealth-in-australia">Can you actually see an IVF dietitian by telehealth in Australia?</h2>
<p>Yes. Dietitians deliver full consultations by telehealth and phone, and this is recognised in the Dietitians Australia <a href="https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/advocacy-and-policy/position-statements/telehealth-position-statement">telehealth position statement</a> as a way of improving access to care, especially for people facing geographic or other barriers.</p>
<p>Many Australians cannot see a dietitian in person because of distance or access barriers, which is exactly where telehealth helps. This is relevant for readers outside Brisbane or in regional areas who still want fertility nutrition support from a qualified professional.</p>
<p>A telehealth consult involves the same core work as an in-person visit. It includes history taking, medical and cycle context, supplement review, and a written plan sent afterwards. After a first consult with me you get a written plan within 2 business days, and we review supplements every 6 to 12 weeks. The <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7540717/">Dietitians Australia position statement</a> confirms that telehealth can deliver high-quality dietetic services when practitioners follow appropriate clinical standards.</p>
<h2 id="what-does-an-ivf-dietitian-actually-do-in-the-months-before-a-cycle">What does an IVF dietitian actually do in the months before a cycle?</h2>
<p>The months before an IVF cycle are where most of the useful nutrition work happens, and it is the window people most often skip past. I think of this as the time to get on the front foot and build a good baseline diet, rather than making rushed changes once a cycle has already begun.</p>
<p>The evidence base is honest and clear. Diet-and-fertility research is largely observational, and it points to overall eating patterns, such as Mediterranean-style eating, rather than any single hero food. Katrina Ridout cites Gaskins and Chavarro&#39;s 2018 review in the American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology on this point.</p>
<p>A preconception consult typically reviews current intake, supplements, caffeine, alcohol, and omega-3s. Around 200 mg of caffeine a day is described as a sensible cap, with one coffee at roughly 100 mg. The NHMRC advises that not drinking alcohol is safest when planning a pregnancy. Omega-3s from fish two to three times a week are also discussed. A written plan and individualised supplement plan follow the first consult, with supplements reviewed every 6 to 12 weeks.</p>
<h2 id="how-does-telehealth-fertility-nutrition-work-alongside-your-ivf-clinic">How does telehealth fertility nutrition work alongside your IVF clinic?</h2>
<p>Dietetic support sits alongside, and never replaces, medical or fertility-clinic care. Your dietitian works with the timeline and instructions your clinic has given you, not in place of them.</p>
<p>In practice, you bring your cycle timeline and clinic instructions to the consult, and the nutrition plan is built around those dates. This coordination means your eating and supplement routine supports what your medical team is already doing.</p>
<p>No referral is needed to book with a dietitian, though a Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan. Partners can be included in the process, since sperm take around three months to develop and sperm health is influenced by overall eating pattern, alcohol, smoking, sleep and activity. The <a href="https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/advocacy-and-policy/position-statements/telehealth-position-statement">Dietitians Australia telehealth position statement</a> supports this collaborative model of care.</p>
<h2 id="what-should-you-check-before-choosing-a-telehealth-fertility-dietitian">What should you check before choosing a telehealth fertility dietitian?</h2>
<p>Verify the APD credential through Dietitians Australia. APD status confirms university-level training and ongoing professional development, which matters when you are making decisions about supplements and eating patterns during fertility treatment.</p>
<p>Ask how the dietitian communicates with your fertility clinic and GP, and what written documentation you receive after each consult. A written plan is a practical record you can refer back to between appointments.</p>
<p>Ask which telehealth mode is used, whether online or by phone, and how follow-up reviews are spaced. The <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7540717/">Dietitians Australia position statement</a> notes that telehealth should be chosen to suit the client&#39;s needs and circumstances. A short introductory call can help you judge fit before committing to a longer engagement.</p>
<h2 id="how-i-work-with-you-for-ivf-preparation-by-telehealth">How I work with you for IVF preparation by telehealth</h2>
<p>Before our first consult I&#8217;ll ask for your recent bloods, photos of your supplements (front and back of the pack, so I can see at a glance what&#8217;s in them) and a jotted typical day. No stress if it&#8217;s rough. Then we spend 75 minutes going through your history, your cycle timeline and your clinic&#8217;s instructions, and I send you a written plan and an individualised supplement plan within 2 business days.</p>
<p>I don&#8217;t supplement willy-nilly. We use supplements to supplement your diet, not replace it, so I only add what your food can&#8217;t cover or what the evidence clearly supports. From there it&#8217;s simple little switches, not a diet you&#8217;re on for three months and then off. If something in the plan isn&#8217;t working, we tweak it.</p>

<h2 id="does-telehealth-nutrition-support-suit-everyone-planning-for-ivf">Does telehealth nutrition support suit everyone planning for IVF?</h2>
<p>It depends. Telehealth suits many people well, particularly readers outside Brisbane, those balancing clinic appointments and work, and those who prefer consulting from home. The <a href="https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/advocacy-and-policy/position-statements/telehealth-position-statement">Dietitians Australia position statement</a> highlights that telehealth improves access for people who face geographic or other barriers to in-person care.</p>
<p>In-person care may be preferable when hands-on assessment or local coordination is needed. Katrina Ridout offers both in-person consults in Brisbane and telehealth Australia-wide, so the choice can be based on your circumstances rather than availability.</p>
<p>Nutrition support is complementary and does not promise any outcome. The aim is to support your overall health through the IVF process, whatever that process looks like for you.</p>
<h2 id="what-are-the-limits-of-the-evidence-on-diet-and-ivf-outcomes">What are the limits of the evidence on diet and IVF outcomes?</h2>
<p>Most diet-fertility research is observational, showing associations rather than causation. It does not establish that any eating pattern improves IVF success rates. This is an important distinction when you are weighing up advice from different sources.</p>
<p>What a dietitian can reasonably do is improve overall diet quality, address nutrient gaps, review supplement safety and dosing, and support gut symptoms where relevant. For example, a structured low FODMAP approach may help gut symptoms in endometriosis but does not treat the endometriosis itself. Katrina Ridout cites Monash University for how the low FODMAP approach works.</p>
<p>Individual advice should be tailored to your history and cycle. Questions about your cycle, medications, and IVF protocol belong with your fertility clinic, not your dietitian.</p>
