If you have endometriosis and you are thinking about pregnancy, you have probably already been handed a list. Cut gluten. Cut dairy. Cut red meat. Try this supplement, then that one. The lists arrive with enormous confidence and very little context, and most of them were not written for someone who is also trying to conceive.

I am an Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) working in fertility and pregnancy nutrition. I am a fertility dietitian in Brisbane and I see clients across Australia by telehealth. Endometriosis comes up constantly in that work, usually alongside a long history of being told contradictory things.

This is a plain description of what nutrition sits alongside in endometriosis care, what it does not replace, and the questions worth bringing to an appointment. It is general education rather than individual advice, and none of it stands in for the care of your GP, gynaecologist or fertility specialist.

## What nutrition can and cannot do

The honest starting point is the boundary. Decisions about imaging, surgery, hormonal therapy and assisted reproduction belong with your medical team. Nutrition does not substitute for any of them, and an eating pattern is not a treatment for endometriosis. Anyone promising otherwise is selling something.

What nutrition work does do is sit beside that care and take on the parts of it that food genuinely touches. In practice that tends to be four things: keeping your overall intake adequate through a demanding and often painful stretch of life; making sure the things that commonly run low, such as iron, are checked by your GP rather than assumed either way; working through the digestive symptoms that so often travel with endometriosis and overlap heavily with irritable bowel symptoms; and laying the nutritional groundwork for a pregnancy before it starts.

That last one is the piece most often missed, and it is the reason endometriosis and fertility nutrition belong in the same conversation rather than in two separate appointments.

## The questions people actually bring

Four come up almost every week.

**Should I cut gluten or dairy?** Some people do find their symptoms settle on a change like this and some find nothing shifts at all. The part worth pausing on is the cost. Removing two major food groups shortly before or during a pregnancy narrows your intake at exactly the point it needs to widen, and it is very hard to undo a restriction once it has become a rule. If a trial is worth running, it is worth running properly, with a plan for what you are watching, how long for, and what you reintroduce.

**Is there an endometriosis diet?** Not in the sense the internet means. There are eating patterns that suit some people better than others, and there is a great deal of research still in progress. There is no single pattern with enough evidence behind it to be handed out as a prescription, and I would be wary of any program that arrives as a rule for everyone.

**What about supplements?** This is worth a conversation rather than a shopping list. Some are sensible preparation for pregnancy under Australian guidance, folate and iodine among them. Others duplicate each other, interact with medication, or simply cost money for no return. Bring what you are taking, including the ones you have half forgotten about, and we can work out what is doing something.

**Does any of this affect my chances of conceiving?** Nutrition is one input among many, and it is not the one that decides the outcome. What it can do is make sure nothing avoidable is working against you, and that you enter a pregnancy well prepared rather than catching up afterwards.

## Endometriosis and the preconception window

The three months or so before conception is the most useful and least used window in fertility nutrition. It is when the groundwork gets done quietly rather than renovated mid-pregnancy, and it is particularly worth using when endometriosis is part of the picture, because so much else is competing for your attention by then.

Practically, that means the routine preconception items handled properly: folate and iodine per the national guidance, iron and vitamin D checked with your GP rather than guessed at, and an eating pattern that already fits your real week before morning sickness and appointments arrive to complicate it.

If you are preparing for IVF, the same window applies and the timelines are usually tighter. There is more on that on the [IVF nutrition](https://www.katridout.com.au/our-services/prenatal-nutrition-and-dietetics/ivf-nutrition/) page, and the broader picture sits on the [nutrition for fertility](https://www.katridout.com.au/our-services/nutrition-for-fertility/) page.

## When it is worth seeing an APD

A few situations where an appointment usually earns its place. When your diet has narrowed over time, whether deliberately through elimination or gradually because eating has become uncomfortable. When bloating and gut symptoms are shaping what you eat day to day. When blood results have come back and nobody has talked you through what they mean for food. When you are preparing for pregnancy or IVF and want the nutrition side settled before you start. And when you have simply collected too much conflicting advice and want someone to help you put most of it down.

In Australia, a GP can sometimes set up a chronic disease management plan, which gives a Medicare rebate on a set number of allied health visits each year, and a dietitian can be one of them. Asking about it is a completely ordinary thing to do.
