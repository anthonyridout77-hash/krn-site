If you’re in the thick of pregnancy nausea, I want you to know this is so common, and it isn’t a sign anything is wrong with your pregnancy. What tends to help most is plain, cold, low-smell food eaten small and often: crackers, toast, cold fruit, yoghurt, smoothies, with a little ginger and some protein where you can manage it. For most women nausea starts around weeks 4 to 6, peaks around weeks 8 to 12 and eases from about week 14. If you can’t keep fluids down, that’s one for your GP or maternity team, not just food changes.

*By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed October 2026.*

## When does morning sickness start and how long does it last?

Nausea usually begins between 4 and 6 weeks of pregnancy, is often at its worst around 8 to 12 weeks, and settles for most women from about week 14 ([Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/dealing-with-morning-sickness)). Most women have some nausea at some point, so you’re far from alone.

The pregnancy hormone hCG peaks around the same time symptoms are worst. Rising oestrogen and progesterone also relax the muscles of your digestive tract, which slows digestion and can leave you feeling queasy.

The name morning sickness is misleading. It can hit at any time of day, and how sick you feel isn’t linked to how well your pregnancy is going. Every pregnancy is different: some women feel a bit off, others struggle to keep anything down.

## What can I eat when nothing sounds appealing?

Plain, higher-carbohydrate, low-fat foods are usually the easiest to face. Bread, rice, crackers and pasta are gentle on a sensitive stomach and give you quick energy ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/vomiting-and-morning-sickness/)).

Bananas, plain rice, stewed apple and toast are a traditional starting point for a queasy stomach. They’re bland, easy to digest and unlikely to set off another wave.

Eat small and often. Five or six small meals across the day usually sit better than three big ones, and an empty stomach tends to make nausea worse ([HealthyWA](https://www.healthywa.wa.gov.au/Articles/J_M/Morning-sickness)). Keep a few crackers by the bed so you can eat something before you get up. It’s prevention, not cure: eating before the nausea builds is much easier than trying to eat once it has.

A lot of everyday comfort foods, like breakfast cereal and bread, are fortified. In Australia, bread-making flour carries added folic acid and iodine, so toast is quietly doing some work for you while your usual vegetables have dropped off.

## Why do cold foods sometimes sit better than hot meals?

Hot food gives off far more aroma than cold food, and smell is often the trigger. A plate of cold fruit, a chilled smoothie or a simple sandwich is much less confronting than a steaming meal.

Cold or room-temperature meals with little smell are a practical tool when cooking smells set you off. Queensland Health’s [Managing morning sickness](https://www.health.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0032/151979/antenatal-mrnsick.pdf) resource suggests exactly this if kitchen smells are overwhelming. If someone else can do the cooking for a few weeks, let them.

Smoothies and soups count too. Liquid meals leave the stomach faster than solid food, and many women find them easier when chewing feels like too much.

## Does ginger actually help with pregnancy nausea?

For many women, yes. Ginger is one of the better-studied food options for pregnancy nausea, and ginger tea, peppermint tea or a little soda water can help settle an upset stomach ([Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/dealing-with-morning-sickness)). Ginger biscuits, grated fresh ginger in hot water or a ginger lolly are all easy ways in.

If you’re thinking about ginger supplements, check the dose with your GP, midwife or dietitian first. Food amounts are generally fine, but supplement doses deserve individual advice.

## Can protein and vitamin B6-rich foods ease nausea?

Some early research suggests protein-rich snacks may settle nausea a little better than carbohydrate on its own. Pairing a small serve of protein with your crackers or toast (a bit of cheese, yoghurt, a boiled egg, a handful of nuts) also keeps you going for longer. Pregnancy protein needs sit at around 60 grams a day (NHMRC Nutrient Reference Values), but in the worst weeks, just get in what you can.

Vitamin B6 can ease nausea for some women. Talk to your GP or pharmacist before starting a supplement, especially if you’re already taking a pregnancy multivitamin. B6-rich foods like wholegrains, bananas, chicken and fish are worth including when you can tolerate them.

Eggs are a handy protein choice, and the choline in the yolk supports your baby’s brain development. In pregnancy, cook them until the yolk is firm.

## Which foods and drinks to go easy on while you feel nauseous?

Fried and fatty foods take longer to leave the stomach and are more likely to make nausea worse. When your digestion has already slowed, a heavy meal can sit uncomfortably and bring on another wave.

Usual pregnancy food safety still applies. Deli meats and other higher Listeria risk foods are only okay if heated until steaming hot, and unpasteurised dairy and juices, and fish high in mercury, are not recommended during pregnancy ([Queensland Health](https://www.health.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0032/151979/antenatal-mrnsick.pdf)).

Diet soft drinks and sweeteners add little nutrition, so when your appetite is small, give the space to foods that bring something with them.

## When should I get help for morning sickness instead of trying more food tips?

Contact your GP or maternity team if you can’t keep fluids down, you’re losing weight, or you feel dizzy, faint or unusually tired. Severe vomiting in pregnancy (hyperemesis gravidarum) needs medical assessment and treatment, and food changes alone aren’t enough at that point ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/vomiting-and-morning-sickness/), [Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/dealing-with-morning-sickness)). Please don’t wait it out.

## Key takeaways

* Morning sickness usually starts between weeks 4 and 6, peaks around weeks 8 to 12, and eases for most women by about week 14.
* It is very common and can happen at any time of day.
* Plain, low-fat, higher-carbohydrate foods such as crackers, toast, rice and pasta are often the easiest to tolerate.
* Eat small and often, and eat before the nausea builds.
* Cold or room-temperature foods smell less than hot meals and are often better tolerated.
* Ginger, and vitamin B6 with your GP’s or pharmacist’s okay, help many women.
* Pair carbs with a little protein when you can.
* See your GP if you can’t keep fluids down, are losing weight, or feel dizzy or faint.
