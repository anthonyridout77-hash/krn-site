If you have PMOS (previously called PCOS) and you’re in Brisbane, you might be wondering whether a dietitian is worth seeing. For a lot of women it really is, because food is one of the most practical tools you have. I see women with PMOS in person in Milton and by telehealth. I guess the key thing is finding someone who builds a plan around your life, not a list of foods you’re never allowed again.

*By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed October 2026.*

## What is PMOS, and why does nutrition matter so much?

PMOS (previously called PCOS) is a common hormonal condition that can affect cycles, skin, hair, weight, mood and fertility. Insulin resistance is part of the picture for many women, and higher insulin can drive the hormonal symptoms. That’s why eating patterns get so much attention. [Jean Hailes](https://www.jeanhailes.org.au/health-a-z/pcos) has a clear overview.

The [2023 international guideline](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/dead156) recommends healthy eating and regular activity as first-line care, and notes there is no single diet that suits everyone. So the plan needs to be yours, not a copy of someone else’s.

## What does a PMOS dietitian actually do?

A PMOS dietitian looks at your history, your bloods, your symptoms and how you really eat across a week. Then we build an eating pattern you can keep up. That usually means looking at the balance of your meals, carbohydrate quality, fibre and protein, and how food is spread across the day.

PMOS care often involves a team: your GP, sometimes a gynaecologist or endocrinologist, and a dietitian. I write back to your doctors so we’re all working in the same direction.

## How does a dietitian approach PMOS nutrition?

It’s about quality and timing, not cutting out food groups. Slower-release carbs like wholegrains, legumes and grainy bread, paired with protein and healthy fats, help keep blood sugar steadier. Spreading carbs across the day rather than one big load helps too.

Add before you subtract: half a plate of veg, a daily handful of berries, fish two to three times a week, and paired snacks. Simple little switches, chosen to fit your budget, culture, cooking time and what you actually enjoy. And if weight is something you’d like to talk about, we can, but it’s not the only measure. Energy, cycles and bloods matter just as much.

## Can a dietitian help with PMOS and fertility at the same time?

Yes. The same work that supports insulin and hormones also supports ovulation, so preconception nutrition, IVF preparation and PMOS care often sit in one plan. We’d also look at folate, iodine, vitamin D and iron before pregnancy, and I err on the side of caution with supplements such as inositol: we check whether they suit you and review them in 6 to 12 weeks.

## What changes can you realistically expect, and when?

Every woman is different. Some notice steadier energy and fewer cravings within a few weeks. Changes in cycles and bloods usually take a few months. Nutrition doesn’t cure PMOS, but it can make a real difference to how you feel day to day, and it sits alongside any medication your doctor recommends.

## How to choose a PMOS dietitian in Brisbane

Look first for the Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) credential. You can check it through [Dietitians Australia](https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/). It only takes a minute, and it tells you the person has a recognised dietetics degree and keeps up ongoing professional development.

Next, look for an evidence-based approach. A good fit will explain the why behind their advice and talk about overall eating patterns rather than hero foods, detoxes or expensive protocols. If something comes with a big promise about outcomes, that promise is usually running ahead of the evidence.

Then think about what matters for you. Ask about experience with PMOS, insulin resistance and fertility, whether they take a food-first approach rather than strict rules, and whether they will work with your GP.

Finally, the practical things count. How long is the first consult? Do you get a written plan? Can your partner come along? Is telehealth an option for reviews? These small details make a big difference to how supported you feel.
