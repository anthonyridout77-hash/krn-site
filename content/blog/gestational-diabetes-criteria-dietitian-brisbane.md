If you are pregnant in Australia now, the glucose test you are offered is being read against different numbers than the ones used a couple of years ago. The test itself has not changed. The thresholds for calling the result gestational diabetes have, and they went up.

By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed November 2026.

This is the sort of change that happens quietly in a guideline document and then takes years to filter through to the people it affects. Nobody sends you a letter about it. So here is what moved, where the numbers came from, and what it does and does not mean for you.

## What actually changed

The Australasian Diabetes in Pregnancy Society, ADIPS, published new consensus recommendations for the screening, diagnosis and classification of gestational diabetes in the Medical Journal of Australia in August 2025. Laboratories were able to start reporting against the new criteria from 1 July 2025.

I wrote an explainer when the criteria first landed, and that piece covers [what the 2025 ADIPS criteria mean](/blogs/gestational-diabetes-test-numbers-changed-adips-2025/) in general terms. This one is narrower: the numbers side by side, and the group the change quietly creates.

Both sets of numbers come from the same test, a 75 gram oral glucose tolerance test.

The previous criteria, set in 2014 when ADIPS adopted the international IADPSG thresholds:

- Fasting plasma glucose 5.1 to 6.9 mmol/L
- One hour plasma glucose 10.0 mmol/L or above
- Two hour plasma glucose 8.5 to 11.0 mmol/L

The 2025 criteria:

- Fasting plasma glucose 5.3 to 6.9 mmol/L
- One hour plasma glucose 10.6 mmol/L or above
- Two hour plasma glucose 9.0 to 11.0 mmol/L

In both versions, one value meeting the criteria is enough for the diagnosis. You do not need all three.

Look at the size of those moves. Fasting went up by 0.2, the one hour by 0.6, the two hour by 0.5. Small numbers that sit right where a lot of results sit.

## Why they were raised

The stated reason is specificity. The 2014 thresholds drew sustained criticism for overdiagnosis, and the 2025 statement describes the change as a modest lift in thresholds intended to improve specificity without compromising safety.

ADIPS is direct about the consequence, and I think it is worth quoting rather than softening. Raising the thresholds at 24 to 28 weeks means some women who would have been diagnosed under the older thresholds will no longer be identified.

That is the intended effect, not a side effect. Whether it is the right trade-off is a live question among clinicians, and I am not going to pretend it is settled.

One figure that gets quoted alongside this change deserves a caution. The 15.3 per cent versus 6.1 per cent prevalence comparison that appears in the consensus statement compares the IADPSG criteria with the older New Zealand criteria in the GEMS trial. It is not a measure of what the 2025 Australian change will do to diagnosis rates. The statement does not quantify that.

## The early pregnancy part most people miss

There is a second half to the 2025 recommendations that gets far less attention than the threshold change, and it matters more for some women.

If you have risk factors for high blood glucose, the recommendation is an HbA1c test in the first trimester. The purpose of that test is to find overt diabetes in pregnancy, which usually means type 2 diabetes that was already there before conception. It is not a gestational diabetes screen, and it is only needed if you have not had HbA1c screening for type 2 diabetes in the previous twelve months.

The criteria for overt diabetes in pregnancy are an HbA1c of 6.5 per cent or above, a fasting plasma glucose of 7.0 mmol/L or above, or a two hour plasma glucose of 11.1 mmol/L or above.

An early glucose tolerance test is routinely recommended in two situations: previous gestational diabetes, or an HbA1c of 6.0 to 6.4 per cent. It may also be considered if you have multiple risk factors, or if your local guidelines call for early screening.

That HbA1c band of 6.0 to 6.4 per cent is the one place in the guidance where food is named directly. Women in that band who have not had a glucose tolerance test should be offered glucose monitoring and dietary education, the same as for a diagnosis of early gestational diabetes.

## Where nutrition sits in all this

Here I want to be careful about scope, because ADIPS is careful about it too. The 2025 statement covers screening, diagnosis and classification. It says plainly that the management of high blood glucose in pregnancy is beyond what the guidance addresses.

So nothing in this document tells you what to eat. What it does is decide who gets identified, and being identified is what opens the door to monitoring and to dietary education.

As general education rather than advice for your situation, the things an Accredited Practising Dietitian looks at in a pregnancy where glucose is a question are fairly ordinary:

- Where the carbohydrate in your day actually sits, which is usually less obvious than people expect
- Whether carbohydrate is arriving on its own or alongside protein and fibre
- How intake is spread across the day rather than concentrated in one or two sittings
- What is realistic when nausea, reflux, work or other children are setting the terms

The second point is the one I end up explaining most often. A piece of toast on its own and the same toast with eggs are not the same event for your body, and the difference is not about eating less.

## If your result sits just under the new line

This is the group the change creates, and there is no guideline written for them.

Under the 2025 thresholds you can have a fasting result of 5.2, or a two hour result of 8.8, and not be diagnosed, where two years ago you would have been. The appointment ends, nothing is flagged, and you go home with a normal result.

A normal result is genuinely good news and I would not undercut it. But if you had been told to expect a diagnosis, or you have had gestational diabetes before, or the number sat close enough that you noticed, it is reasonable to raise that with your GP, midwife or obstetrician rather than filing it away. They have your whole picture, including your [iron and energy](/blogs/iron-in-pregnancy-tired-before-anaemia/) and everything else going on, and they are the right people to decide whether anything further is warranted.

Everybody responds differently to a change in how they eat, and none of this is a prediction about your pregnancy.

## What to do with this

Three practical things.

Ask what your actual numbers were, not just whether you passed. The values matter more than the verdict now that the verdict depends on where a line was drawn.

Ask which criteria your result was read against if your test was in 2025, since 1 July 2025 was when laboratories could begin reporting the new criteria and the change did not land everywhere at once.

If you have had gestational diabetes before, ask about early testing rather than waiting for the standard window, because that is specifically named in the recommendations.
