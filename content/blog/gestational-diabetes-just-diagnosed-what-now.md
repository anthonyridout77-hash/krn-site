If you’ve just been told you have gestational diabetes, I want to start with the thing most women need to hear first: you did not cause this.

I’ve sat with so many women in the days after that phone call. The glucose test comes back, the word “diabetes” lands in the middle of an already emotional pregnancy, and suddenly they’re replaying every piece of birthday cake and wondering what they did wrong. So let’s take a breath and talk about what gestational diabetes actually is, and what the next few months can look like.

## What gestational diabetes actually is

Gestational diabetes, or GDM, is a change in how your body handles blood glucose during pregnancy. As the placenta grows, the hormones it produces naturally make your body less responsive to insulin. For some women, the pancreas can’t quite keep up with the extra demand, and blood glucose levels rise. That’s it. It’s driven by placental hormones, genetics, age and factors far outside your control. It is not a verdict on your diet, your body or your willpower.

You’re also in very good company. Australian national data shows that in 2023-24, almost 1 in 5 women who gave birth were diagnosed with gestational diabetes. Screening has become more thorough and diagnostic criteria have shifted, which means many women who feel perfectly well are picked up early. That’s a good thing, even when it doesn’t feel like it.

## Food is still on your side

The most common fear I hear after diagnosis is that food has become the enemy, or that carbohydrates are now banned. Neither is true.

Australian guidance for GDM starts with gentle, practical eating patterns: regular meals rather than long gaps, carbohydrates spread through the day rather than clustered into one sitting, and leaning towards slower-digesting, lower-GI options where you can. Think of the grainier bread, oats, or legumes you may already enjoy. Pairing carbohydrates with protein and healthy fats also helps many women feel fuller and steadier between meals.

Notice what’s not in that paragraph: nothing about cutting out entire food groups, surviving on salads, or eating a “perfect” day every day. GDM eating, done well, looks a lot like ordinary balanced eating with a bit more structure. Your baby still needs carbohydrate. So do you.

## Your team, and where a dietitian fits

GDM care in Australia is genuinely good. Your obstetrician or midwife, a diabetes educator, and often an endocrinologist will guide monitoring and any medication decisions. Those medical calls always sit with your medical team.

Where I fit, as an Accredited Practising Dietitian, is the food part: turning the clinic handout into meals you actually want to eat, working around nausea, food aversions, a budget, a toddler, or a work schedule, and helping you feel confident rather than anxious at the supermarket. For many women, a couple of unhurried conversations are enough to swap the fear for a plan.

## Be kind to yourself with the numbers

If you’re checking your glucose at home, some readings will sit outside the range even when you’ve done everything “right”. That’s the placenta doing its thing, not you failing. Bring those numbers to your care team, let them adjust the plan, and keep eating in a way you can sustain. A hard week does not undo the good you’re doing every day.

And it’s worth knowing this chapter has an end. For most women, blood glucose settles after the birth, and the follow-up check is simply part of closing the loop.
