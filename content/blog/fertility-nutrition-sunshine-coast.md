<h2 id="what-does-a-fertility-dietitian-actually-do">What does a fertility dietitian actually do?</h2>
<p>A fertility dietitian reviews your overall eating pattern, looks at your blood results and supplements, and coordinates with your medical team during preconception and fertility care. An Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) is university-trained in nutrition and dietetics and is held to the professional standards of Dietitians Australia, so the advice you get is grounded in evidence rather than trends.</p>
<p>Dietitians look at patterns rather than single hero foods. The research on diet and fertility is largely observational, so the focus sits on what you eat most days, not on one miracle ingredient. A balanced eating pattern is associated with better ovulatory function, egg and sperm quality and early pregnancy health.<sup>1</sup></p>
<h2 id="what-does-the-evidence-say-about-diet-and-fertility">What does the evidence say about diet and fertility?</h2>
<p>Research points toward overall eating patterns rather than isolated foods. A Mediterranean-style pattern, rich in vegetables, legumes, wholegrains, fish, nuts and extra virgin olive oil, is the one most often linked with fertility in the studies we have.<sup>1</sup></p>
<p>The nutrients that come up again and again are folate, omega-3 fats, iodine and iron. Folate supports early neural development, which is why a folic acid supplement is recommended for at least one month before conception and through the first trimester.<sup>2</sup> Omega-3 fats come from oily fish, and leafy greens, legumes, nuts, seeds and berries round out the picture. The Australian Dietary Guidelines are a good baseline diet to build from.<sup>3</sup></p>
<p>The evidence shows associations, not promises. No single food or supplement can make a pregnancy happen. What the research does suggest is that a varied, nutrient-dense way of eating gives your body a solid foundation while you work with your fertility clinic and GP.</p>

<p>A telehealth consult is simple: we meet by video, talk through your health and fertility history, look at your current eating and supplements, and agree on a practical plan. Pyjama pants are welcome. We see and hear each other, but you can leave your Ugg boots on.</p>
<p>Some women on the Sunshine Coast prefer to come down to Brisbane for an in-person appointment, and that works too. Either way, the support is the same and it sits alongside your fertility clinic&#39;s care.</p>
<h2 id="what-should-you-look-for-in-a-fertility-dietitian">How to choose a fertility dietitian on the Sunshine Coast</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>The APD credential.</strong> Check that the person is an Accredited Practising Dietitian. You can search for one through Dietitians Australia.<sup>4</sup></li>
<li><strong>Experience in preconception and fertility.</strong> Ask whether they regularly support women trying to conceive, going through IVF, or after a loss.</li>
<li><strong>Familiarity with your conditions.</strong> PMOS (previously called PCOS), endometriosis and thyroid conditions all change the nutrition conversation.<sup>5</sup></li>
<li><strong>They work with your doctors.</strong> A good fit keeps your GP and fertility specialist in the loop and writes back to them.</li>
<li><strong>Telehealth or in person.</strong> Choose the format you will actually keep up with. Consistency matters more than the room you sit in.</li>
<li><strong>Medicare.</strong> Ask your GP whether you are eligible for a Chronic Condition Management Plan. A Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="how-is-fertility-nutrition-tailored-for-ivf-versus-natural-conception">How is fertility nutrition tailored for IVF versus natural conception?</h2>
<p>IVF preparation nutrition sits alongside your fertility clinic&#39;s care and focuses on the months before a cycle, plus support during it. The goal is to build a solid nutritional foundation before treatment begins, then keep it steady while your medical team leads the cycle.</p>
<p>Natural conception support tends to focus on overall eating patterns, cycle regularity and key nutrients over a longer window. It takes about three months to build your eggs, so that runway is worth using well.</p>
<p>An individual supplement plan is part of the work in both paths. Doses should be set with your dietitian or GP rather than taken from forums or comment threads. What suits one person may not suit another, and some supplements interact with medications or medical conditions.</p>
<h2 id="how-does-a-dietitians-approach-differ-from-a-naturopaths-for-fertility">How does a dietitian&#8217;s approach differ from a naturopath&#8217;s for fertility?</h2>
<p>A dietitian&#39;s approach is evidence-based nutrition assessment, a food-pattern focus and coordination with your medical care. An APD reviews what you actually eat, identifies gaps, and makes practical changes that fit your life. The work sits alongside your GP and fertility clinic, not outside them.</p>
<p>Naturopathy may include herbal medicine and other tests or therapies where the evidence is limited, and these are not a substitute for medical fertility care. Whoever you see, ask about their qualifications and the evidence behind their recommendations, and keep your GP and fertility clinic informed about any supplements or herbs you take, because some can interact with fertility medications or affect hormone levels.</p>
