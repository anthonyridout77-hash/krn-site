Most of the nutrition advice a woman reads while she is pregnant stops at the birth. The appointments stop too, or they narrow down to the baby. And yet the months after birth are the ones where the body is rebuilding, sleep is broken, and cooking has to happen with one hand.

This is a plain account of what I look at as a postpartum dietitian in Brisbane when someone comes in a few weeks or a few months after having a baby. It is general education rather than personal advice, and none of it replaces the care of your GP, midwife or obstetrician.

## What actually changes after birth

Two things change at once, and they pull in opposite directions. Your nutrient needs do not drop back to pre-pregnancy levels, and in some cases they go up. At the same time, the practical capacity to shop, cook and sit down to eat falls to its lowest point in years.

That gap is the whole problem. It is rarely solved by learning more about nutrition. It is usually solved by changing what food is physically available in the house at four in the afternoon.

So the first question I ask is not what you should be eating. It is what you actually ate yesterday, and what got in the way.

## Breastfeeding, energy and fluid

Breastfeeding uses a considerable amount of energy and nutrients, as the Victorian Government’s Better Health Channel puts it. There is no single kilojoule figure that fits everyone, and chasing one is not especially useful. What is useful is noticing whether meals are happening at all, or whether the day has quietly become four coffees and whatever was left on the bench.

Fluid is the simplest thing to get right. Better Health Channel suggests expecting to drink up to two litres a day while breastfeeding, and that water is the best source. A glass at every feed is an easier rule to keep than a daily total.

Calcium needs stay high. The Australian guidance is around two to three serves of calcium-rich food a day for adult women, and four serves if you are under eighteen.

## Iodine is the one supplement with a clear national recommendation

Iodine is the nutrient most people are surprised by, because the requirement is higher after the birth than during the pregnancy.

The NHMRC Nutrient Reference Values set the recommended dietary intake for iodine at 270 micrograms a day while breastfeeding, compared with 220 micrograms a day in pregnancy and 150 micrograms a day for other adult women.

Separately, the National Health and Medical Research Council recommends that all women who are pregnant, breastfeeding or considering pregnancy take an iodine supplement of 150 micrograms each day. Women with an existing thyroid condition are advised to speak with their medical practitioner before starting one.

That is worth knowing because plenty of women stop their pregnancy supplement on the day they give birth, which is the point at which the iodine requirement is at its highest.

## Iron, and why the months after birth are when stores get rebuilt

Pregnancy draws down iron stores. Better Health Channel says so plainly, and recommends rebuilding them afterwards through foods such as red meat and legumes.

What that means in practice is that the postpartum period is the repair window, not the depletion window. Iron-rich food eaten regularly does more over three months than any single heroic meal does in a week. Pairing iron-rich food with a source of vitamin C at the same meal, such as tomato in a beef dish or citrus alongside lentils, helps with absorption.

The part I would push hardest on is this. If you had a blood test in late pregnancy or after the birth, ask what the result actually said, and ask whether anyone intends to recheck it. Bringing that number to an appointment changes the conversation completely.

## Eating when you have one hand and no time

![A woman eating a bowl of cereal with milk at home](/images/wp/2026/09/krn-postpartum-one-handed-eating-body.jpg)

A meal that needs no assembly beats a better meal that never gets made.

This is the section that does the most work in a real consultation.

* Cook once and portion for later. A one-pot meal that makes six serves is worth more than six different recipes.
* Keep food that needs no assembly within reach of where you feed. Tinned fish, cheese, fruit, yoghurt, nuts, wholegrain crackers.
* Decide the evening meal in the morning, while you still have the capacity to decide anything.
* Add before you subtract. In these months, taking food away almost never helps.
* Accept help in the form of food. It is the most useful thing anyone can bring.

## What you do not have to do

![A woman at home eating a slice of wholegrain toast with a nut butter spread](/images/wp/2026/09/krn-postpartum-iron-rich-meal-body.jpg)

Caffeine and ordinary food do not have to go. Most of what gets cut in these months did not need cutting.

A surprising amount of postpartum food advice circulating online is not supported by evidence, and it adds a layer of worry to a period that has enough of it.

On foods supposedly upsetting a breastfed baby, Better Health Channel is direct: there is little evidence that certain foods upset babies or give them diarrhoea or colic. Blanket elimination diets while breastfeeding are worth discussing with a professional before starting, because they can narrow intake at exactly the wrong time.

Caffeine does not have to go. Better Health Channel suggests limiting it to three or fewer caffeinated drinks a day. On alcohol, its position is that not drinking is the safest choice.

## When it is worth seeing an Accredited Practising Dietitian

Not everyone needs one. It is worth considering if iron or another result has come back low and you are not sure what to do with it, if you are breastfeeding while excluding a food group for any reason, if intake has dropped away and fatigue is not shifting, or if you are simply eating nothing that resembles a meal and want a plan that survives a bad night.
