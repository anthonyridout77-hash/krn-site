If you’re trying to conceive — or preparing for IVF — you’ve probably come across claims that you can “improve egg quality naturally.” But is this actually true? And if so, what does the evidence say works?

As a fertility dietitian, this is one of the questions I get asked most often. So let’s look at what we actually know — and separate evidence-based strategies from marketing hype.

## Can You Really Improve Egg Quality?

The short answer: yes, to a meaningful degree — especially in the 90–120 days before an egg is released or retrieved. This is the window during which follicular development occurs, and nutritional status during this period directly influences the health of the oocyte.

You can’t change your genetic material or turn back the biological clock. But you can create the best possible nutritional environment for the eggs you have — and the research shows this makes a real difference in outcomes, especially in IVF.

## Antioxidants: The Foundation of Egg Quality Nutrition

Oxidative stress — an imbalance between free radicals and the antioxidants that neutralise them — is one of the primary mechanisms that damages egg quality. The follicular fluid surrounding each egg is rich in antioxidants, and this environment is directly influenced by your diet.

**Key antioxidant nutrients for egg quality include:**

* **Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)** — A mitochondrial antioxidant that plays a direct role in cellular Some research, including a number of small RCTs, suggests CoQ10 supplementation (200–600mg/day) may support egg quality markers and fertilisation rates in IVF, particularly in women over 35 or with diminished ovarian reserve, though evidence in this area is still developing.
* **Vitamin C and E** — Work synergistically to reduce oxidative damage. Found in colourful vegetables, citrus, nuts, and seeds.
* **Alpha-lipoic acid** — Regenerates other antioxidants and supports mitochondrial function. Limited but promising evidence in reproductive contexts.
* **Selenium and zinc** — Critical for antioxidant enzyme systems. Deficiency of either is associated with poor reproductive outcomes.

## Omega-3 Fatty Acids and Follicular Health

DHA and EPA — the long-chain omega-3 fatty acids found in oily fish and fish oil supplements — have been shown in observational studies to be positively associated with oocyte quality and embryo morphology. They work by:

* Reducing inflammatory signalling in follicular fluid
* Improving cell membrane fluidity (critical for fertilisation)
* Supporting luteal phase progesterone production

Aim for 2–3 serves of oily fish per week (salmon, sardines, mackerel) or 1–2g DHA+EPA from a quality supplement daily.

## The Mediterranean Diet Pattern

The Mediterranean dietary pattern — emphasising vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, fish, and moderate dairy — is the most consistently evidence-supported eating pattern for female fertility. A 2018 study published in *Human Reproduction* found that women adhering to a Mediterranean-style diet had significantly higher IVF success rates.

This pattern works not through any single “superfood” but through its collective anti-inflammatory, antioxidant-rich, and insulin-sensitising effects.

## What About Myo-Inositol?

Myo-inositol has strong evidence for improving egg quality specifically in women with PCOS — it improves insulin sensitivity, reduces androgen levels, and has been shown in RCTs to improve oocyte quality and IVF outcomes in this group. For women with PCOS, it’s one of the most evidence-supported supplements available. For women without PCOS, evidence is more limited but still promising.

## Vitamin D and Egg Quality

Vitamin D deficiency is extremely common in Australia — despite our sunshine — particularly in women who are indoors a lot, or who cover up for sun protection reasons. Vitamin D receptors are present in ovarian tissue, and deficiency is associated with poorer IVF outcomes, lower fertilisation rates, and reduced clinical pregnancy rates. A simple blood test can check your status; optimal levels for fertility are typically 75–100 nmol/L.

## Folate vs. Methylfolate: Which Should You Take?

Folate (or its active form, methylfolate/5-MTHF) is essential for DNA synthesis and cell division — processes central to egg maturation and early embryo development. Standard prenatal supplements contain folic acid (synthetic folate), but women with MTHFR gene variants may not convert folic acid efficiently. A [fertility dietitian in Brisbane](https://www.katridout.com.au/our-services/nutrition-for-fertility/) can assess whether you need the methylated form based on your history and, if available, genetic testing.

## What Doesn’t Work (Despite the Hype)

Not everything marketed to fertility patients has solid evidence behind it. Some products frequently overhyped include:

* **DHEA** — Evidence primarily in women with diminished ovarian reserve; not recommended for general use without specialist input
* **Melatonin** — Preliminary evidence only; timing and dosing are critical and not well established
* **Excessive vitamin megadosing** — More is not always better; some nutrients (e.g. vitamin A) are teratogenic in high doses

## How Long Does It Take?

Meaningful improvements in egg quality from nutritional changes take a minimum of 90 days — the full duration of follicular development. This is why I recommend women begin optimising their nutrition at least 3 months before trying to conceive or starting IVF stimulation.
