If you’re trying to conceive, chances are your feed has quietly filled up with gut-health content. Microbiome testing kits. Probiotic “fertility stacks.” Thirty plants a week challenges. Gut resets. It’s a lot — and when you’re already carrying the emotional load of TTC, the last thing you need is another list of rules you’re apparently getting wrong.

So let’s take a breath together, and I’ll share what the research actually says — and what I’d gently suggest instead of an expensive overhaul.

## What the science says (honestly)

The gut microbiome — the trillions of microbes living in your digestive system — is one of the most exciting areas in nutrition research right now, and that includes reproductive health. Researchers are exploring how the gut may interact with hormones, nutrient absorption and the body’s inflammatory balance, all of which matter in the preconception window.

But here’s the honest bit you won’t see in the ads: this research is still emerging. We don’t yet have evidence that changing your gut bacteria will improve your chances of conceiving, and anyone promising that is ahead of the science. What we *do* know with confidence is that the eating patterns that support a healthy gut — plenty of plant variety, fibre, and everyday fermented foods — are the same patterns that support your overall health while you’re trying to conceive. That’s a lovely two-for-one, with no gimmicks required.

![Fresh vegetable and bean salad in a glass bowl](/images/wp/2026/07/krn-gut-health-fertility-nutrition-body1-1.jpg)

A bowl full of colour is one of the kindest things you can do for your gut.

## Start with colour, not perfection

The single most supported idea in gut health is beautifully simple: your gut microbes thrive on variety, especially plant variety. Different vegetables, fruits, wholegrains, legumes, nuts and seeds each feed different microbes.

In the middle of a Brisbane winter, that doesn’t need to mean cold salads. Think roasted pumpkin and chickpeas through a warm couscous, a lentil and vegetable soup, extra veg stirred through a slow-cooked casserole, or a handful of frozen peas and corn thrown into almost anything. Frozen and tinned vegetables absolutely count — they’re picked at their peak and they make variety achievable on tired weeks.

If counting “30 plants a week” feels motivating, lovely. If it feels like homework, please let it go. “A little more colour than last week” is a perfectly good goal.

## Fibre: the quiet achiever

Fibre is what most of our gut microbes actually eat, and most Australian women get less than recommended. Gentle ways in: swap to wholegrain or seeded bread, add a tablespoon of chia or linseed to your porridge, keep the skin on your potatoes and apples, and let legumes sneak into soups and bologneses. Small, boring, brilliant — and if your tummy is sensitive, build up slowly and be kind to yourself along the way.

## Fermented foods, without the fuss

Everyday fermented foods — yoghurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso — are a lovely, food-first way to bring friendly microbes to the table. A dollop of yoghurt on your porridge or a small glass of kefir is plenty; nothing exotic required. If dairy isn’t your thing, there are options we can chat through together.

## What I’d gently skip

Expensive microbiome tests and big probiotic supplement stacks bought off social media. Not because supplements never have a place — sometimes they genuinely do — but because they should be chosen for *you*, based on your health history and your bloods, ideally with your GP or dietitian alongside you. Please don’t spend hundreds of dollars because an influencer told you to.

![Woman relaxing at home with a warm drink](/images/wp/2026/07/krn-gut-health-fertility-nutrition-body2-v3.jpg)

Gentle, unhurried changes tend to be the ones that last.

## The kindest takeaway

Your gut doesn’t need to be perfect for you to be “doing TTC right.” A bit more colour, a bit more fibre, some everyday fermented foods, and a lot less pressure — that’s a gut-friendly, fertility-friendly, *you*-friendly place to start.
