<p>Coffee and wine come up a lot when we talk about trying to conceive, so let&#8217;s keep it simple. The current Australian guidance is to keep caffeine to around 200 mg a day, which is roughly two coffees, while you are trying to conceive. For alcohol, the guidance is not to drink at all while you are planning a pregnancy, because no safe amount has been established. This is general information, not individual advice, so it&#8217;s always worth talking through your own situation with your GP or fertility specialist.</p>

<h2 id="what-does-the-evidence-say-about-caffeine-and-fertility">What does the evidence say about caffeine and fertility?</h2>
<p>The evidence on caffeine and fertility is largely observational, so it points at patterns rather than proving cause and effect. A 2017 systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis investigated coffee and caffeine intake in relation to time to pregnancy and spontaneous abortion among women with natural fertility. The same review also examined clinical pregnancy rate and live birth rate among women in fertility treatment.</p>
<p>The review pooled data from case-control and cohort studies, with literature searches conducted in MEDLINE and Embase and no time or language restrictions. This broad approach means the findings draw on a wide range of study designs and populations, which strengthens the overall picture even though individual studies can vary in quality.</p>
<p>According to <a href="https://www.yourfertility.org.au/everyone/drugs-chemicals/caffeine">Your Fertility</a>, there is no clear proof that caffeine affects the ability to become pregnant. However, some studies have found that women who drink large amounts of caffeine may take longer to become pregnant and may have a higher risk of miscarriage.</p>
<p>The key point is that the research identifies associations, not direct causation. Women who consume high amounts of caffeine may also have other habits or circumstances that influence fertility, and observational studies cannot fully separate those factors.</p>
<h2 id="how-much-caffeine-is-safe-when-trying-to-conceive">How much caffeine is safe when trying to conceive?</h2>
<p>The practical recommendation from <a href="https://www.yourfertility.org.au/everyone/drugs-chemicals/caffeine">Your Fertility</a> is to limit daily caffeine intake to no more than 200 mg per day, which is approximately two cups of coffee, if you are pregnant or trying to conceive. This figure gives you a clear reference point without requiring you to cut out caffeine completely.</p>
<p>A cup of coffee averages about 85 mg of caffeine, but the actual amount ranges from 40 mg to 175 mg depending on the bean type and how the coffee is prepared. That range means &quot;two cups&quot; is a rough guide rather than a precise measure. A strong barista-made coffee could contain far more caffeine than a weak instant coffee.</p>
<p>The 2017 <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5733907/">systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis</a> reported a significantly increased risk of spontaneous abortion at 300 mg of caffeine per day. That threshold sits well above the 200 mg daily limit, which supports the idea that staying within the recommended range is a sensible middle ground.</p>
<p>Caffeine also comes from tea, cola, energy drinks and chocolate, so it is worth counting the whole day rather than coffee alone. A morning coffee, an afternoon tea and a chocolate bar can add up quickly.</p>
<h2 id="does-caffeine-increase-the-risk-of-miscarriage">Does caffeine increase the risk of miscarriage?</h2>
<p>The 2017 <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5733907/">systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis</a> found that coffee and caffeine consumption was associated with a significantly increased risk of spontaneous abortion at 300 mg of caffeine per day. That finding comes from pooled data across multiple studies, which gives it more weight than any single study alone.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.yourfertility.org.au/everyone/drugs-chemicals/caffeine">Your Fertility</a> also lists a slightly higher risk of miscarriage among women who drink large amounts of caffeine. The word &quot;slightly&quot; matters here. The increased risk is not dramatic, and it is most relevant to intakes well above the recommended limit.</p>
<p>It is important to be clear that this is an association, not proof that caffeine causes miscarriage. Many factors contribute to miscarriage risk, and observational research cannot isolate caffeine as the single cause.</p>
<p>Staying within the 200 mg daily guidance is a practical way to act on the uncertainty without over-restricting. You do not need to eliminate coffee entirely to take a reasonable, evidence-informed step.</p>
<h2 id="can-caffeine-affect-male-fertility-or-sperm-quality">Can caffeine affect male fertility or sperm quality?</h2>
<p>Yes, high doses of caffeine might affect sperm quality, but the evidence is not strong enough to say whether this affects men&#39;s fertility. According to <a href="https://www.yourfertility.org.au/everyone/drugs-chemicals/caffeine">Your Fertility</a>, the research on caffeine and male fertility is less clear than the research on women.</p>
<p>The evidence base for men is thinner, and there is no specific milligram limit for men trying to conceive in the current guidance. That does not mean caffeine is irrelevant for male fertility, but it does mean there is no precise number to aim for.</p>
<p>A sensible approach for partners is to keep caffeine intake moderate and consistent with general healthy eating rather than chasing a specific number. Cutting back on very high intakes, such as multiple energy drinks or many strong coffees each day, is reasonable without being overly restrictive.</p>
<p>Your partner is very welcome to join a consult. I guess the key thing is that it&#8217;s a team effort, so it makes sense for both of you to be more intentional for a few months.</p>
<h2 id="what-about-alcohol-when-trying-to-conceive">What about alcohol when trying to conceive?</h2>
<p>The safest approach is not to drink while you are trying to conceive. The <a href="https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/health-advice/alcohol">NHMRC Australian guidelines to reduce health risks from drinking alcohol</a> say that to reduce the risk of harm to their unborn child, women who are pregnant or planning a pregnancy should not drink alcohol.</p>
<p>Unlike caffeine, there is no daily amount that is considered risk-free during the preconception period. Many women stop when they start trying, because you often won&#8217;t know you&#8217;re pregnant for the first few weeks.</p>
<p>If that feels like a big change, swap rather than stop. Soda water with fresh lime and mint in your favourite glass still feels like a Friday night ritual. Simple little switches like this are much easier to stick with than willpower alone.</p>
<h2 id="does-alcohol-combined-with-caffeine-affect-conception">Does alcohol combined with caffeine affect conception?</h2>
<p>The combined effect of alcohol and caffeine on conception hasn&#8217;t been well studied, so there is no clear finding to share. What we do know is that each is worth moderating on its own.</p>
<p>Keeping caffeine within the 200 mg daily limit and not drinking alcohol are sensible steps whether or not the two interact.</p>
<p>If you have questions about your own intake, bring them to your GP or fertility specialist. Individual advice should come from a health professional who knows your full history.</p>
