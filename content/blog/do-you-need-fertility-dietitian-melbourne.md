If you are in Melbourne and trying to conceive, you might be wondering whether you need a fertility dietitian close by. You don’t. I work with women right across Australia by telehealth from Brisbane, and I guess the key thing is choosing someone with the right qualification and a way of working that suits you, rather than someone in your postcode. Nutrition supports your GP and fertility clinic. It never replaces them, and no diet can promise a pregnancy.

*By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed October 2026.*

## What does a fertility dietitian actually do?

A fertility dietitian is an Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) who works with women and couples before conception. The advice is individual, built on university training, rather than a trending list of fertility foods.

A first consult usually covers your cycle and health history, any diagnoses such as PMOS (previously called PCOS) or endometriosis, what your fertility clinic has planned, your recent bloods, supplements and how you really eat across a week. From there you get a plan that fits your life and sits alongside your medical care.

## What should you eat in the months before trying to conceive?

The months before conception are where most of the useful work happens. The research points to overall eating patterns rather than single foods: plenty of vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, legumes, nuts, fish and olive oil, the Mediterranean-style pattern that shows up again and again in reviews of diet and fertility ([Gaskins and Chavarro](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajog.2017.10.003)).

Folate and iodine are the two supplements Australian advice recommends before you fall pregnant, and [Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/planning-for-pregnancy) has a good overview. Beyond that, I look at iron, vitamin D, B12 and omega-3s, because these are the nutrition gaps I see most often, and I check them against your bloods rather than guessing.

Fish two to three times a week, a daily handful of berries and half a plate of veg at lunch and dinner are simple little switches that cover a lot of ground. It’s not rocket science, and it doesn’t need to be perfect.

## Does male fertility nutrition matter too?

Yes. Sperm take around three months to develop, so the months before conception count for partners too. Overall eating pattern, alcohol, smoking, sleep and activity all play a part. The [NHMRC alcohol guidelines](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/health-advice/alcohol) are clear that not drinking is safest when you are planning a pregnancy, and I talk about that with both partners.

I often see couples together, because it is so much easier to make changes when you are both eating from the same pot.

## How does nutrition fit alongside IVF, PMOS and endometriosis?

Each of these changes the conversation. With PMOS, insulin resistance is often part of the picture, so we look at pairing carbs with protein and fibre and spreading them across the day. With endometriosis, gut symptoms are common, and a structured low FODMAP approach may help those symptoms, though it doesn’t treat the endometriosis itself.

With IVF, timing matters. The months before a cycle are the time for changes, and the stimulation weeks are the time to keep things simple and steady. A general nutrient list only takes you so far, which is why individual advice matters here.

## How to choose a fertility dietitian in Melbourne

Look first for the Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) credential. You can check it through [Dietitians Australia](https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/). It only takes a minute, and it tells you the person has a recognised dietetics degree and keeps up ongoing professional development.

Next, look for an evidence-based approach. A good fit will explain the why behind their advice and talk about overall eating patterns rather than hero foods, detoxes or expensive protocols. If something comes with a big promise about outcomes, that promise is usually running ahead of the evidence.

Then think about what matters for you. If you have PMOS, endometriosis, a thyroid condition, or you are heading into IVF, it helps to see someone who knows those areas well. Ask whether the dietitian works with these regularly, and whether they will talk to your doctors.

Finally, the practical things count. How long is the first consult? Do you get a written plan? Can your partner come along? Is telehealth an option for reviews? These small details make a big difference to how supported you feel.
