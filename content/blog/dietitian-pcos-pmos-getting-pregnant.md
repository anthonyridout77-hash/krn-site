**Quick answer:** Yes. The 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline for PCOS (now also called PMOS) recommends healthy eating and physical activity as first-line care, including when trying to conceive. An Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) helps by improving insulin resistance, supporting regular ovulation, and preparing nutrient stores for pregnancy. This works alongside, not instead of, your GP or fertility specialist. The guideline says no single “PCOS diet” is best. What works is a plan you can keep.

*By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed September 2026.*

## PCOS is now PMOS

PMOS is the newer name for PCOS. It reflects that the condition is metabolic and hormonal, not just about the ovaries. You’ll still see “PCOS” on most websites, reports and blood-test forms. They’re the same condition.

## How nutrition affects fertility with PMOS

Most people with PMOS have some degree of insulin resistance, whatever their weight, which can raise androgen levels and disrupt ovulation. For those with a higher weight, the guideline notes that even modest weight change (5–10%) can improve ovulation. But weight isn’t the only target. Eating quality, regular meals and activity matter at any size.

## What a fertility dietitian actually does

* **Assesses** your cycles, blood tests, medications (for example metformin or letrozole), eating pattern and lifestyle.
* **Builds an eating plan** based on lower-GI carbohydrates, protein at each meal, fibre and healthy fats, which helps steady blood glucose.
* **Reviews supplements.** Folic acid, iodine, vitamin D if low, and an evidence-based look at inositol. Read [inositol and PCOS: what the 2026 evidence says](https://www.katridout.com.au/blogs/inositol-pcos-what-the-2026-evidence-says/).
* **Works with your team:** your GP, gynaecologist or fertility clinic, including before IVF.
* **Avoids harm.** No crash diets, which can worsen disordered eating risk. People with PMOS face a higher risk of disordered eating.

## What the evidence does not support

* One “PCOS diet” (keto, dairy-free or gluten-free) being superior for everyone
* Detoxes or cleanses
* Expensive supplement stacks without testing

## FAQ

**Can I get pregnant with PCOS or PMOS?**  
Many people with PMOS conceive, some naturally and some with medical help. Nutrition supports your chances but doesn’t guarantee an outcome.

**Do I need to lose weight to get pregnant with PCOS?**  
Not necessarily. For some people, modest weight change improves ovulation. Your dietitian focuses on sustainable habits rather than a number.

**Should I see a dietitian before IVF with PCOS?**  
Yes. Three months before a cycle is a useful window.
