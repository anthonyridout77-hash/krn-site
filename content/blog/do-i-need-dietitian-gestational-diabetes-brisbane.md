If you’ve just come back from your glucose test with a gestational diabetes diagnosis, you might be wondering whether you really need a dietitian, or whether a handout from the clinic will do. I guess the key thing is this: food is the first part of managing gestational diabetes, and a dietitian helps you shape it around your own readings, your appetite and your actual week. You don’t have to work it all out on your own, and it’s not about cutting out everything you love. Nutrition sits alongside your obstetrician or midwife, diabetes educator and GP, never in place of them.

## What is gestational diabetes, and why was I tested?

Gestational diabetes happens when pregnancy hormones make insulin work less efficiently, so blood sugar rises above the target range. It is not something you did or ate. It’s a hormonal shift that some bodies handle more easily than others, especially when there’s existing insulin resistance, a family history, or PMOS (previously called PCOS) in the picture.

It’s common, affecting around 1 in 10 pregnancies in Australia, and it’s usually picked up on the routine oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) between 24 and 28 weeks, according to [Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/gestational-diabetes). Women with risk factors may be tested earlier. Most women feel completely well and have no idea their levels are running high, which is exactly why the screening matters.

In Australia, a fasting reading of 5.1 mmol/L or above, a 1-hour reading of 10.0 mmol/L or above, or a 2-hour reading of 8.5 mmol/L or above meets the criteria for gestational diabetes. It’s different from type 1 diabetes, where the body doesn’t make insulin, and type 2 diabetes, where the body doesn’t use insulin well or doesn’t make enough.

## What are the possible complications for me and my baby?

The possible complications are real, but they are risks, not certainties, and good management changes the picture. For mum, gestational diabetes can increase the chance of preeclampsia, a caesarean birth, and type 2 diabetes or heart disease later in life.

For baby, it can mean growing larger than average (macrosomia), low blood sugar after birth, breathing difficulties and jaundice, along with a higher chance of weight and blood sugar problems later on. That list can feel scary on a fact sheet. The reason your care team acts quickly is that keeping your levels in range lowers the chance of each one.

Gestational diabetes usually goes away after the birth, which is lovely to hear when you’re in the thick of it. You’ll be offered a follow-up glucose test after baby arrives, and regular checks with your GP after that, so any change is picked up early.

## How is gestational diabetes managed with food and lifestyle?

Food and lifestyle are first-line care, and many women keep their levels in range this way, with or without medication. The pillars are your eating pattern, regular movement, checking your blood glucose, and insulin or tablets under medical supervision if they’re needed.

Carbohydrate is the nutrient that raises blood sugar most directly, so the aim is not to remove it but to spread it across the day in amounts your body can handle. Pregnancy is not the time for a low or no carb diet. Pairing your carbs with protein, fibre and a little healthy fat slows the rise and keeps you fuller for longer. The [Australian Dietary Guidelines](https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/) are still the foundation underneath it all.

There’s no single gestational diabetes diet, and targets are individual. What keeps one woman’s readings steady may not work for the next, so a plan built around your readings, your appetite, your kitchen and your cultural foods will always beat a printed sheet.

## Will I have to follow a strict diet?

No. This isn’t about cutting out whole food groups or never eating carbohydrate again. In practice it’s regular meals, sensible portions of carbs spread through the day, and enough protein and fibre to keep things steady. A rhythm, not a punishment.

Treats and family foods can usually be worked in with a bit of planning. A bowl of rice at a family dinner or a slice of birthday cake can often fit when the rest of the day is balanced around it. It’s about learning how your body responds, then adjusting the portion or the timing.

If you work shifts, chase other kids around, or rely on quick meals, your plan needs to reflect that. The strategies that stick are the ones you’ll still be using at 36 weeks.

## What does a gestational diabetes dietitian actually do?

A dietitian starts with your history, your blood glucose readings, how you eat now, and anything else in the picture, like insulin resistance, family history or PMOS. That first appointment is about understanding what your body is doing and where the easy wins are, never a lecture about what you could have done differently.

When you’re choosing someone, look for an Accredited Practising Dietitian. You can check the credential through [Dietitians Australia](https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/find-an-apd). Ask whether they’ll work with your obstetrician or midwife and diabetes educator, and whether you’ll leave with something written down, because it’s very hard to remember everything when you’re tired and a bit overwhelmed.

## How many appointments will I need, and can I do them online?

It depends on your readings, how confident you feel, and whether medication becomes part of your care. Many women have a first appointment and then a few reviews through the rest of the pregnancy, with more support if things change.

Telehealth works really well for gestational diabetes. You can sit at your kitchen bench with your meter and talk through the week’s numbers without driving across town in the third trimester. You don’t need a referral to see a dietitian, and a Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan, so it’s worth asking your GP.

## Does gestational diabetes mean I’ll get type 2 diabetes later?

It raises the chance for both you and your child, but it’s not a certainty. Think of the diagnosis as a signal that your body finds insulin a little harder to manage, and a signal worth acting on. It doesn’t mean diabetes is waiting for you.

The months and years after birth are a real window. The simple little switches you made in pregnancy can become a foundation rather than a memory, and breastfeeding and good postpartum nutrition help too.

## Key Takeaways

* Gestational diabetes affects around 1 in 10 pregnancies in Australia and is usually picked up on the OGTT between 24 and 28 weeks.
* It’s caused by pregnancy hormones affecting insulin, not by anything you did wrong.
* Food and lifestyle are first-line care, with medication added if needed.
* There’s no single strict diet; the amount, type and timing of carbs are tailored to your readings.
* Gestational diabetes usually resolves after birth, but follow-up glucose checks matter.
* No referral is needed, and a Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan.
