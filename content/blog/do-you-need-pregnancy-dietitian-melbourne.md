If you are pregnant in Melbourne and thinking about seeing a pregnancy dietitian, you have more choice than you might think. Telehealth means you can work with an Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) anywhere in Australia, and I see lots of women from Victoria from my clinic in Brisbane. Nutrition supports your GP, obstetrician and midwife. It never replaces them.

*By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed October 2026.*

## What does a pregnancy dietitian actually do?

A pregnancy dietitian gives you individual nutrition support across preconception, pregnancy and the postpartum months. In a first appointment I look at your medical history, how you are eating now, your supplements and any symptoms you are juggling.

The everyday topics are often nausea and vomiting, iron and folate, iodine, vitamin D, fibre and bowel health, food safety, and eating well with twins. The job is to translate the clinical advice from your medical team into food you will actually eat on a busy weekday.

## Why look beyond your hospital for nutrition support?

Hospital dietitian services can be excellent, but they are usually reserved for women with a specific referral from within the hospital, and appointments are set around their schedule. Many women want continuity from preconception right through to the postpartum months, and they want to choose when they are seen.

Telehealth is a practical answer if you are already juggling work, scans and blood tests. No commute, no parking, and you can still share your results and talk everything through just as you would in a clinic room.

## How does nutrition change across each trimester?

In the first trimester, folate and iodine are central because they support early development. Nausea and food aversions often shape what is realistic, and that’s totally valid. Small, frequent, plain meals are fine for now. [Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/planning-for-pregnancy) covers the supplement basics.

In the second trimester, blood volume rises, so iron, calcium and vitamin D become more important, and reflux and changing appetite are common. Gestational diabetes screening usually happens between 24 and 28 weeks.

In the third trimester, fibre and fluids matter more for bowel comfort, and smaller meals help with reflux. There is no single pregnancy plate that suits everyone, so I adjust the plan to your symptoms and blood results.

## Can a dietitian help with gestational diabetes, PMOS or endometriosis?

Yes. Gestational diabetes is one of the most common reasons women see me in pregnancy, and the plan is about the right type and amount of carbohydrate, spread across the day and paired with protein and fibre ([Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/gestational-diabetes)).

PMOS (previously called PCOS) and endometriosis care often starts well before a positive test, in the preconception window. Nutrition supports the management of these conditions alongside your medical team. It doesn’t cure them, and I’m always honest about that.

## How to choose a pregnancy dietitian in Melbourne

Look first for the Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) credential. You can check it through [Dietitians Australia](https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/). It only takes a minute, and it tells you the person has a recognised dietetics degree and keeps up ongoing professional development.

Next, look for an evidence-based approach. A good fit will explain the why behind their advice and talk about overall eating patterns rather than hero foods, detoxes or expensive protocols. If something comes with a big promise about outcomes, that promise is usually running ahead of the evidence.

Then think about what matters for you. If you have gestational diabetes, PMOS, severe nausea, a vegetarian diet or a twin pregnancy, look for someone who sees these often and takes a food-first approach rather than calorie counting. Ask how often they see this, and whether they will talk to your doctors.

Finally, the practical things count. How long is the first consult? Do you get a written plan? Can your partner come along? Is telehealth an option for reviews? These small details make a big difference to how supported you feel.
