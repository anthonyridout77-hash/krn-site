By four months, the story most of us are told is that things settle. The baby sleeps a little longer, the fog lifts, and you start to feel like yourself again. If that has not happened for you, and you are still dragging yourself through each day, it is reasonable to ask why rather than to assume you simply have to wait it out.

Tiredness after birth has more than one cause. Broken sleep is the obvious one. Iron is one of the less obvious ones, and it is worth understanding because unlike sleep it can actually be measured. This is a conversation I have often as a postnatal fatigue dietitian, and it usually starts with a woman apologising for making a fuss about being tired.

## The tiredness everyone expects, and the tiredness worth asking about

Some postnatal tiredness is simply the arithmetic of interrupted sleep, and no amount of nutrition will out-run a 3am feed. That kind of tiredness lifts when you get a decent stretch of sleep, even a single one.

The tiredness worth asking about behaves differently. It does not lift after a good night. It can come with breathlessness on the stairs you used to take without thinking, a heart that races when you stand up, cold hands, headaches, or hair coming out in the shower. Some women describe it as feeling wrung out rather than sleepy.

None of those signs diagnoses anything on its own, and there are several possible explanations for each of them, including thyroid changes and mood. That is exactly why the useful next step is a conversation with your GP rather than a guess, or a supplement bought on the strength of a symptom list.

## What iron does after birth, and why blood loss at delivery matters months later

Iron sits at the centre of haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from your lungs to everywhere else. When iron stores fall, oxygen delivery becomes less efficient, and the body reports that as fatigue long before anything shows up as anaemia on a full blood count.

Two things happen around birth that draw on those stores. The first is pregnancy itself, which increases iron requirements substantially in the third trimester as your blood volume expands and your baby builds their own stores. The second is blood loss at delivery, which happens to some degree in every birth and to a greater degree in some.

What surprises people is the lag. Stores drawn down in the third trimester and at delivery do not necessarily announce themselves in week two, when you are running on adrenaline and everyone expects you to be tired anyway. They can show up at three or four months, when the adrenaline has gone and you were promised you would feel better. If you want the pregnancy side of this story, I have written separately about [iron in pregnancy and feeling tired before anaemia shows up](https://www.katridout.com.au/blogs/iron-in-pregnancy-tired-before-anaemia/).

## Food sources that actually shift iron, and what blocks absorption

Not all dietary iron is equal, and this is where a lot of well-meant advice goes wrong.

![Plates of lentils, spinach, mixed beans, pumpkin seeds and wholegrain bread, all sources of iron](/images/wp/2026/08/krn-postnatal-iron-rich-foods.jpg)

### Haem iron

Found in red meat, lamb, kangaroo, chicken thigh, and in oysters and sardines. Your body absorbs this form far more readily than the plant form, and absorption is not much affected by what else is on the plate. A modest serve of red meat two or three times a week does more work than a very large salad.

### Non-haem iron

Found in lentils, chickpeas, kidney beans, tofu, pumpkin seeds, cashews, wholegrain bread and iron-fortified breakfast cereals. Spinach appears on every list, though the iron in it is poorly absorbed, so it is a useful food rather than the solution it is often presented as.

Non-haem iron is absorbed better in the presence of vitamin C. That is not a supplement instruction, it is a plate instruction. Tomato through the lentils, capsicum in the stir fry, a squeeze of lemon over the beans, or a piece of fruit alongside the fortified cereal.

### What blocks absorption

Tea and coffee contain compounds that bind iron and reduce how much you take up from a meal. If you are drinking tea with breakfast and again with lunch, moving those cups to between meals rather than with them costs nothing and is one of the few genuinely free changes available. Calcium competes with iron too, so a large glass of milk alongside your main iron-containing meal is working against you, whereas the same milk at another time is not.

## Where supplements fit, and where they do not

Iron supplements are not a wellbeing purchase, and they are not something to start on the basis of feeling tired. Taking iron you do not need is not neutral. It can cause constipation and nausea, which is the last thing you need in the postnatal period, and iron loading can itself be harmful.

The right order is a blood test first, then a decision made with your GP about whether to supplement, at what dose, and for how long. Dose and frequency both matter, and neither is something to work out from a label on a box.

Food and supplements are not competing options. If your bloods show depleted stores, food alone will usually not refill them quickly, and that is not a failure of your diet. Food is what holds the level once it has been restored, and what slows the drift in the first place.

## What to ask your GP for

Going in with specific questions tends to produce a more useful appointment than going in with “I am exhausted”, which is easy for anyone to hear as ordinary new-parent tiredness.

* Ask for a **full blood count and a ferritin level**. Ferritin reflects your stores, and stores fall before the full blood count changes, so a normal full blood count on its own does not close the question.
* Ask what your ferritin number actually was, not just whether it was normal. Reference ranges are wide, and the bottom of a range is a different situation from the middle of it.
* Mention the blood loss at your birth if you know roughly what it was, and whether you were treated for low iron during the pregnancy.
* Ask whether anything else should be checked at the same time. Thyroid function and vitamin B12 come up often in this conversation.
* If you are breastfeeding, say so, because it affects which options are on the table.

If your bloods come back unremarkable and you still feel the way you feel, that is information rather than a dead end. It moves the question elsewhere, and it is worth going back rather than concluding you were imagining it.

## The short version

* Tiredness that does not lift after a good night’s sleep is worth investigating rather than waiting out.
* Iron stores drawn down in pregnancy and at delivery can show up months later, not immediately.
* Haem iron from meat and seafood is absorbed more readily than plant iron. Vitamin C helps plant iron along, tea, coffee and calcium hold it back.
* Do not start iron supplements on symptoms alone. Ask your GP for a full blood count *and* a ferritin level, and ask what the ferritin number was.
* Food holds your levels steady. It is not usually enough to refill depleted stores on its own, and that is not your fault.
