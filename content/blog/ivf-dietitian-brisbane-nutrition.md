If you are preparing for IVF, you have probably been handed a great deal of advice about food. Some of it came from your clinic, some from a forum at eleven at night, and some from a stranger in a comment thread who was very sure of herself. It is genuinely hard to tell which part deserves your energy, at a time when your energy is already spoken for.

So here is what an Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) actually looks at when someone comes in before or during a cycle. Not a protocol, and not a promise. A short list of things that are worth attention, and an honest account of where nutrition sits alongside your medical care.

## The months before a cycle

This is where most of the useful work happens, and it is the window people most often skip past. The research that exists on diet and fertility is largely observational, and it points at overall eating patterns rather than at any single hero food. Patterns built around vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fish, nuts and olive oil come up repeatedly. No one food in that list is doing the work.

What a dietitian is actually checking in this window is less glamorous than a superfood list. Are you eating enough, and often enough? Is iron adequate, particularly if your periods are heavy or you eat little red meat? Is B12 covered if you are vegetarian or vegan? Has vitamin D ever been looked at? Your fertility or maternity team will advise on folic acid and iodine supplementation, which is standard Australian preconception advice, and that conversation belongs with them rather than with an internet list.

The other thing worth naming here is that this is also the window where people are most vulnerable to being sold something. If a protocol costs several hundred dollars and comes with a strong claim about outcomes, that claim is almost certainly running ahead of the evidence.

## The stimulation weeks

Mid-cycle is the wrong moment to overhaul how you eat. Appetite can be unpredictable, bloating is common, and many people find they feel full quickly and then ravenous an hour later. A new set of food rules dropped on top of that tends to add strain rather than remove it.

What tends to help is unremarkable and practical. Eat regularly rather than waiting until you are empty. Keep protein in the picture across the day, because it is the thing people quietly drop when they are not hungry. Keep fluids up. Have a few low-effort options in the freezer for the evenings when you have been at the clinic since seven and cooking is not going to happen.

## The transfer and the two week wait

There is nothing you need to overhaul here either, which is a difficult sentence to sit with when you badly want something to do. The two week wait is hard, and food is one of the few levers that feels like it is within reach, so it attracts a lot of anxious attention.

This is a sensible point to settle your food safety habits, since they will matter if the cycle works: the usual Australian advice about listeria-risk foods, reheating leftovers until they are steaming hot, and being careful with pre-prepared salads and cold ready-to-eat meats. On alcohol, the Australian guidance is unambiguous for anyone pregnant or planning a pregnancy, and it is simply not to drink.

## What nutrition cannot do

It cannot make a cycle work. No food, supplement or eating pattern has been shown to do that, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling you something. Saying so plainly matters, because the flip side of an overstated claim is the quiet belief afterwards that a failed cycle was somehow a failure of diet. It was not.

What nutrition can reasonably contribute is narrower and still worth having. It can make sure you are not going into a demanding process depleted. It can steady your energy through weeks that ask a lot of you. It can take a few decisions off your plate at a point when decisions are expensive. And it can stop you spending money and hope on something with nothing behind it.

## When it is worth seeing an APD

Not everyone preparing for IVF needs a dietitian. It is worth a conversation if any of these are part of your picture: PMOS, formerly known as PCOS; endometriosis; a previous pregnancy affected by gestational diabetes; low iron or B12; coeliac disease or another condition that limits what you can eat; a long history of restrictive eating; recent or current GLP-1 medication; or food aversions severe enough that whole groups have dropped out of your week.
