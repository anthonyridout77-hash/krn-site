Being told you have gestational diabetes can feel like a lot to take in, and the first question most women ask me is “so what can I actually eat?” The good news is that a gestational diabetes meal plan is not a low-carb diet. I guess the key thing is to eat small and often: three smaller meals and two to three snacks, each one pairing a low-GI carbohydrate with protein and fibre, so your glucose gets a gentle trickle rather than a big load at once. Food is one part of your care team’s plan, alongside glucose monitoring and medical review, and here’s what it looks like day to day in an Australian kitchen.

## What does a gestational diabetes meal plan actually look like each day?

A gestational diabetes meal plan is built around three smaller meals and two to three snacks rather than three big meals. Spreading carbohydrate across the day helps keep blood glucose steadier, because your body gets it in smaller, more manageable amounts. When it comes to glucose, we want boring average wherever we can.

As rough guideposts, many women start with around 30 to 45 grams of carbohydrate (two to three serves) at each main meal, and 15 to 30 grams (one to two serves) at snacks. One serve is about 15 grams, which might look like one slice of bread, half a cup of cooked pasta, or a small piece of fruit. These numbers are a starting structure only. Your dietitian or diabetes educator will tailor your portions to your body, your activity and your glucose readings.

Pregnancy is not the time for a low or no carbohydrate diet. Carbohydrate still fuels you and your baby, and cutting it out can leave you tired and missing key nutrients. The aim is to choose it carefully and spread it evenly, according to the [National Diabetes Services Scheme (NDSS)](https://www.ndss.com.au/wp-content/uploads/resources/booklet-gestational-diabetes-caring-for-yourself-and-baby.pdf).

There is also no need to eat for two. Your needs rise a little in the second and third trimester, and the extra is small and concrete: a pouch of yoghurt, an extra piece of fruit, or a bigger serve of vegetables is usually enough.

## Which foods should I put on my plate, and which should I limit?

Build each meal around three things: a low-GI carbohydrate, a protein food and plenty of vegetables. Aim for half a plate of vegetables or salad at your main meals. The protein might be eggs, chicken, fish, tofu, legumes or lean meat, and the low-GI carbohydrate could be grainy bread, basmati rice, sweet potato, pasta or legumes. Pairing the carbohydrate with protein and fibre slows down how quickly it hits your bloodstream.

Fruit is still part of a gestational diabetes eating pattern. Around two serves a day works for most women, choosing lower-GI options such as apples, pears, citrus and stone fruit, and spreading them across the day rather than having both at once, as the [NDSS gestational diabetes eating guide](https://gdandme.ndss.com.au/eating-well/) explains.

Some foods and drinks raise blood glucose quickly without offering much nutrition: lollies, regular cordial, juice and soft drink, regular jelly, honey, jam, syrups, biscuits, cakes, pastries, ice cream, chocolate, chips and takeaway foods. These aren’t banned. They’re once-in-a-blue-moon foods rather than everyday ones, and when birthday cake comes around, have a small piece and enjoy it.

Small amounts of sugar do not greatly affect blood glucose. A teaspoon of sugar in a hot drink is generally fine, and intense sweeteners approved in Australia, such as stevia, sucralose and aspartame, are considered safe in pregnancy in normal amounts.

## How do I plan a week of meals without overhauling my whole life?

A lot of nutrition comes down to planning. A practical week follows a simple rhythm: three meals and two snacks a day, built on low to medium GI foods that are higher in fibre and lower in saturated fat.

A sample day might start with two slices of grainy toast with eggs and spinach, which gives roughly 30 grams of carbohydrate. Morning tea could be a small tub of yoghurt with a handful of berries. Lunch might be a wholegrain wrap with chicken, salad and avocado, followed by an afternoon snack of cheese and wholegrain crackers. Dinner could be a stir-fry with basmati rice, tofu and plenty of vegetables. Make a double batch and take the leftovers for lunch the next day, and suddenly you have two balanced meals.

A simple weekly structure takes the decision fatigue away. Choose two breakfast defaults you enjoy, rotate three or four dinners, and keep a short list of grab and go snacks in the fridge and your bag. We’re creatures of habit, so make the decisions once, then follow the template through the week.

Remember that any plan is a starting point, not a prescription to copy exactly. If it’s not working, we tweak it based on your glucose readings and your appetite.

## Which foods should I avoid during pregnancy for food safety?

Some foods are avoided in pregnancy because of food poisoning risk, and this is separate from glucose management. These include soft cheeses such as brie, camembert, ricotta, feta and blue cheese (unless cooked and eaten hot), unpasteurised dairy, cold deli meats, store-bought sushi, bean sprouts, pre-prepared salads, pâté, raw eggs, alcohol, and fish high in mercury, according to [Pregnancy, Birth and Baby](https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/pregnancy/nutrition-and-lifestyle/foods-to-avoid-when-pregnant).

These foods can carry listeria, salmonella or other infections that are more serious in pregnancy, whether or not you have gestational diabetes. Reheat leftovers until they are steaming hot all the way through, including the meals you take to work. I’ve written more on this in [what foods to avoid during pregnancy in Australia](https://www.katridout.com.au/blogs/foods-to-avoid-during-pregnancy-australia/).

## When should I check in with my care team about my levels?

Check in with your care team whenever your glucose readings are consistently outside the targets your team has set, or when you notice a pattern you can’t explain. Nutrition is one lever. Your team looks at your monitoring, growth scans and how you’re feeling to decide whether more support, including medication, is needed. Needing medication is not a failure. Sometimes the placenta’s hormones simply win, and that’s no reflection on how hard you’re trying.

Steady glucose matters because levels that stay high can make the baby grow larger than expected, can make birth harder, and can lead to low blood sugar for bubs after birth, according to the [NDSS](https://www.ndss.com.au/about-diabetes/gestational-diabetes/gestational-diabetes-faqs/). Gestational diabetes also raises the chance of type 2 diabetes later, which is why follow-up after birth matters.

Bring your food and glucose records to appointments so changes are based on your actual week, not guesswork. Your records show patterns a single reading can’t. Don’t beat yourself up over one high number. You’re not a robot, and you’re growing a tiny human.

* A gestational diabetes meal plan spreads carbohydrate across three smaller meals and two to three snacks rather than three big meals.
* Rough guideposts are 30 to 45 grams of carbohydrate at main meals and 15 to 30 grams at snacks, where one serve is about 15 grams, tailored to your readings.
* Pregnancy is not the time for a low or no carbohydrate diet; choose carbohydrate carefully and spread it evenly.
* Pair each carbohydrate with protein and fibre, and aim for half a plate of vegetables at main meals.
