When you’re trying to conceive and you also have a thyroid condition, it can feel like one more thing to juggle. I guess the key thing is this: your thyroid and your reproductive hormones talk to each other, so looking after one supports the other. No single food or supplement treats a thyroid disorder, and food never replaces your medication. What good nutrition can do is close the nutrition gaps, keep your supplements safe around your tablets, and give your medical care a good baseline diet to work with.

*By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed October 2026.*

## How does your thyroid affect fertility?

Your thyroid gland makes two main hormones, thyroxine (T4) and tri-iodothyronine (T3). The numbers refer to how many iodine atoms each hormone carries, which is why iodine matters so much. These hormones set how your body uses energy, and they also interact with your reproductive hormones.

Thyroid conditions are common in women of reproductive age. Hyperthyroidism is when the thyroid makes too much hormone, and the most common cause is Graves' disease, an autoimmune condition. Hypothyroidism is when it makes too little, often because of Hashimoto's thyroiditis, another autoimmune condition ([Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/hyperthyroidism); [Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/hypothyroidism)).

Both can lead to irregular periods and problems ovulating, and thyroid conditions that aren't well managed are linked with a higher risk of miscarriage ([Alexander et al., Thyroid, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2016.0457)).

This is why your GP or fertility specialist checks thyroid function as part of preconception and fertility care. Nutrition sits alongside that medical care, supporting your overall health while your medical team manages your thyroid condition.

## Which nutrients actually matter for thyroid function when you're trying to conceive?

Iodine is the nutrient most directly tied to thyroid function, because your thyroid needs it to make T4 and T3. Adult women need about 150 micrograms a day, rising to 220 micrograms in pregnancy and 270 micrograms while breastfeeding ([NHMRC Nutrient Reference Values](https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/nutrient-reference-values/nutrients/iodine)). Food sources include iodised salt, seafood, dairy, eggs and Australian bread, which is made with iodised salt. Your own needs depend on your diagnosis.

Vitamin D may also be relevant, as low levels are common in people with autoimmune thyroid conditions. The research is still developing, so think of it as a reminder to have your vitamin D checked with your GP rather than a reason to start a high-dose supplement.

Selenium, zinc and omega-3 fats come up a lot in thyroid forums, but the evidence for supplements is limited. There is no specific food or supplement that treats a thyroid disorder, and supplements should never replace prescribed medication.

## Should you avoid goitrogenic foods like broccoli, cabbage and soy?

No, you don't need to cut these foods out. Goitrogens are compounds that can interfere with how the thyroid uses iodine, but this mainly matters when someone is already low in iodine.

They're found in cruciferous vegetables such as broccoli, cauliflower and cabbage, as well as soy and millet. Cooking reduces the effect in cruciferous vegetables, and you would need to eat very large amounts before it became a real concern. Under normal eating the risk is very low, so keep enjoying your veg. Half a plate of it is still the goal.

Soy is slightly different because it can reduce how well levothyroxine is absorbed. Your doctor or pharmacist will tell you how far apart to keep soy foods and your tablet, and a gap of about four hours is common advice. Variety and preparation matter more than avoiding these foods altogether.

## Is iodine supplementation safe if you have a thyroid condition?

It depends on your diagnosis. The NHMRC recommends iodine supplements for women who are pregnant, breastfeeding or planning a pregnancy, but says women with a pre-existing thyroid condition should talk to their doctor before taking one ([NHMRC](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/about-us/publications/iodine-supplementation-pregnant-and-breastfeeding-women)). With an overactive thyroid such as Graves' disease, extra iodine can make things worse.

Kelp and other seaweed supplements are worth leaving on the shelf. They can contain very large and variable amounts of iodine, which can upset thyroid function.

Supplement needs differ depending on whether you have hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism or an autoimmune thyroid condition. Individual advice from your doctor or dietitian is safer than following forum recommendations. I err on the side of caution here.

## How should you time thyroid medication around food and supplements?

Thyroid medication is usually taken on an empty stomach, and some supplements, particularly iron and calcium, can stop it being absorbed properly. A gap of about four hours between your tablet and those supplements is common advice, but follow what your doctor or pharmacist tells you for your own medication ([Alexander et al., Thyroid, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2016.0457)).

This matters a lot in preconception, because most pregnancy multivitamins contain iron and iodine. A simple routine, like your thyroid tablet first thing and your multivitamin with lunch or dinner, makes it easy to get right every day.

Keep your prescribing doctor informed of anything you add, including supplements, herbal products or big changes to how you eat. Your dose may need adjusting over time, and it often changes in pregnancy.

## What else supports thyroid health while you're planning a pregnancy?

An overactive thyroid that isn't well controlled can weaken bones over time ([Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/hyperthyroidism)), so thyroid management matters for long-term health as well as fertility. Calcium-rich foods and regular movement help here too.

Have thyroid function checked and monitored by your GP or specialist before and during preconception planning. Regular blood tests help your medical team adjust medication as your needs change.

Research on diet and fertility in thyroid disease is still limited. Most guidance is about overall eating patterns rather than single foods, so a varied, nourishing diet is the practical foundation.

## Key Takeaways

* Both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism can cause irregular periods and ovulation, and poorly managed thyroid conditions are linked with a higher miscarriage risk.
* Graves' disease is the most common cause of an overactive thyroid; Hashimoto's thyroiditis is a common cause of an underactive one.
* Women need about 150 micrograms of iodine a day, more in pregnancy and breastfeeding, but talk to your doctor before taking iodine if you have a thyroid condition.
* Goitrogens in cruciferous vegetables, soy and millet mainly matter alongside low iodine, and cooking reduces the effect in vegetables.
* Keep iron and calcium supplements, and soy, a few hours away from your thyroid tablet, as your doctor or pharmacist advises.
* No specific food or supplement treats a thyroid disorder; nutrition supports, and never replaces, medical care.
