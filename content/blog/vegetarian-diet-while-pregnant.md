<p>Yes, you can absolutely follow a vegetarian diet while you&#8217;re pregnant. I guess the key thing is that it needs a little more planning, not a whole new way of eating. A 2019 review of the research concluded that well-balanced vegetarian and vegan diets can be considered safe for mum and baby in pregnancy (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6470702/">Sebastiani et al., Nutrients, 2019</a>). The four nutrients I&#8217;d want you to be more intentional about are iron, protein, vitamin B12 and iodine. This is general information, not individual advice, and it sits alongside your antenatal care, never in place of it.</p>
<p><em>By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed October 2026.</em></p>

<h2 id="what-nutrients-matter-most-in-a-vegetarian-pregnancy-diet">What nutrients matter most in a vegetarian pregnancy diet?</h2>
<p>Pregnancy lifts your needs for iron, iodine, folate and protein, so a vegetarian diet works well when it&#8217;s planned rather than left to chance. The four to watch most closely are iron, protein, vitamin B12 and iodine. Omega-3 fats and vitamin D are worth knowing about too.</p>
<p>The Australian Dietary Guidelines suggest around 5 serves of vegetables, 2 of fruit, 8½ of grain foods (mostly wholegrain), 2½ of dairy or alternatives and 3½ of the lean meat and alternatives group each day in pregnancy (<a href="https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/food-essentials/how-much-do-we-need-each-day/recommended-number-serves-adults">Eat for Health, NHMRC</a>). If you&#8217;re vegetarian, legumes, tofu, eggs, nuts and fortified milks fill that alternatives group.</p>
<p>Nobody&#8217;s got a perfect diet. Variety and balance do the heavy lifting, and a good baseline diet built on whole foods you actually enjoy is something you can keep up for the whole nine months.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-you-get-enough-iron-on-a-vegetarian-pregnancy-diet">How do you get enough iron on a vegetarian pregnancy diet?</h2>
<p>Iron from plant foods isn&#8217;t absorbed as easily as iron from animal foods, so it pays to be deliberate (<a href="https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/food-essentials/five-food-groups/lean-meat-and-poultry-fish-eggs-tofu-nuts-and-seeds-and">Eat for Health, NHMRC</a>). That doesn&#8217;t mean plant iron isn&#8217;t good enough. It means what you pair it with matters.</p>
<p>A standard serve from this food group is 1 cup (150g) of cooked or canned legumes, 170g of tofu, 2 large eggs or 30g of nuts or seeds (<a href="https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/food-essentials/five-food-groups/lean-meat-and-poultry-fish-eggs-tofu-nuts-and-seeds-and">Eat for Health, NHMRC</a>). Legumes are the workhorse here, so try to get them in most days.</p>
<p>Pair your iron foods with vitamin C at the same meal. Vitamin C from fruit and vegetables helps your body absorb iron from plant sources. An orange after lunch, capsicum in a stir-fry or tomato through a lentil sauce all count. A lentil bolognese, a tofu and capsicum stir-fry or scrambled eggs with spinach are simple little switches that do two jobs at once.</p>
<p>Think of your ferritin like your reserve tank. If your bloods show it&#8217;s running low, that&#8217;s something to sort with your GP early, on the front foot rather than the back foot.</p>
<h2 id="how-much-protein-do-you-need-in-pregnancy-and-where-does-it-come-from">How much protein do you need in pregnancy, and where does it come from?</h2>
<p>Aim for a protein food at every meal. It spreads your intake across the day and makes your target far easier to reach.</p>
<p>The lean meat and alternatives group is the protein anchor in Australian pregnancy guidance, at around 3½ serves a day, and legumes, tofu, eggs, nuts and seeds do the work for vegetarians (<a href="https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/food-essentials/five-food-groups/lean-meat-and-poultry-fish-eggs-tofu-nuts-and-seeds-and">Eat for Health, NHMRC</a>). A practical spread might be legumes at lunch, tofu or eggs at dinner, and nuts with yoghurt or a fortified soy milk as snacks. Pair protein, carb and fibre in those snacks and they&#8217;ll have much more staying power.</p>
<h2 id="do-you-need-a-vitamin-b12-supplement-if-you-dont-eat-animal-foods">Do you need a vitamin B12 supplement if you don&#8217;t eat animal foods?</h2>
<p>If you eat no animal foods at all, yes, you&#8217;ll very likely need one. Vitamin B12 is found naturally only in animal foods, so people who don&#8217;t eat them may need a supplement (<a href="https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/food-essentials/five-food-groups/lean-meat-and-poultry-fish-eggs-tofu-nuts-and-seeds-and">Eat for Health, NHMRC</a>). B12 matters for your baby&#8217;s brain and nerve development. If you eat eggs and dairy you&#8217;ll get some, but it&#8217;s still worth checking.</p>
<p>Ask your GP about checking your B12 and the right dose for you, in pregnancy and while breastfeeding. The need carries on after birth.</p>
<p>A pregnancy multivitamin is a sensible base, but check how much B12 it actually contains rather than assuming it covers you. We use supplements to supplement your diet, never to replace it.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-you-meet-iodine-needs-without-eating-fish">How do you meet iodine needs without eating fish?</h2>
<p>The NHMRC recommends that all women who are pregnant, breastfeeding or planning a pregnancy take an iodine supplement of 150 micrograms a day (<a href="https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/about-us/publications/iodine-supplementation-pregnant-and-breastfeeding-women">NHMRC</a>). Iodine is one of the clearest cases where a supplement, not just food, does part of the job.</p>
<p>Some plant milks are fortified with iodine and some aren&#8217;t, so read the label. Australian bread made with regular flour also carries iodine, but gluten-free bread doesn&#8217;t have to.</p>
<p>Skip kelp or seaweed tablets. The amount of iodine in them varies a lot, so a standard pregnancy supplement with iodine is the safer, more predictable option (<a href="https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/about-us/publications/iodine-supplementation-pregnant-and-breastfeeding-women">NHMRC</a>).</p>
<h2 id="what-about-omega-3-vitamin-d-and-food-safety-on-a-vegetarian-diet">What about omega-3, vitamin D and food safety on a vegetarian diet?</h2>
<p>Your body converts only a small amount of the plant omega-3 in foods like linseed, chia and walnuts into the long-chain omega-3 that oily fish provides. If you don&#8217;t eat fish, an algae-based omega-3 supplement is an option worth talking through with your GP or dietitian, and check it&#8217;s suitable for pregnancy and free of added vitamin A.</p>
<p>Vitamin D comes mostly from sunlight. If you get little sun, cover up for cultural reasons or have darker skin, ask your GP whether your vitamin D should be checked.</p>
<p>Food safety still applies on a vegetarian diet: soft and semi-soft cheeses like brie, camembert and ricotta from the deli, unpasteurised dairy, pre-cut melon and bagged salads sitting in the fridge are on the list to avoid or eat freshly cooked (<a href="https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/pregnancy/nutrition-and-lifestyle/foods-to-avoid-when-pregnant">Pregnancy, Birth and Baby</a>).</p>
<h2 id="is-a-vegetarian-pregnancy-diet-safe-for-you-and-your-baby">Is a vegetarian pregnancy diet safe for you and your baby?</h2>
<p>Yes. The 2019 review found that well-balanced vegetarian and vegan diets can be considered safe for mothers and babies in pregnancy (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6470702/">Sebastiani et al., Nutrients, 2019</a>). &#8220;Well-balanced&#8221; is the important bit. The safety finding rests on planning, the right supplements where they&#8217;re needed, and variety across the food groups.</p>
<p>Start a folic acid supplement of at least 400 micrograms a day at least a month before you try to conceive and keep going through the first three months (<a href="https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/healthy-diet-during-pregnancy">Pregnancy, Birth and Baby</a>). That one&#8217;s for everyone, not just vegetarians. Regular check-ups with your GP, midwife or obstetrician stay central, and always be guided by your GP or specialist.</p>

<h2 id="key-takeaways">Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>A well-balanced vegetarian diet can be considered safe in pregnancy for mum and baby.</li>
<li>Plant iron is absorbed less easily, so pair iron foods with vitamin C at the same meal.</li>
<li>Aim for around 3½ serves a day from the lean meat and alternatives group, with legumes, tofu, eggs and nuts.</li>
<li>If you eat no animal foods, you will very likely need a vitamin B12 supplement; ask your GP.</li>
<li>Take an iodine supplement of 150 micrograms a day and skip kelp tablets.</li>

</ul>
