<p>Yes, you can, if you have a chronic health condition and your GP has set up a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan (GPCCMP). I get asked this all the time, so let me walk you through it simply. The plan gives you a set number of Medicare-subsidised visits with an Accredited Practising Dietitian each year, and you pay the gap between the dietitian&#8217;s fee and the rebate. I guess the key thing is that the rebate isn&#8217;t automatic: the plan needs to be in place before you book, and nutrition support always works alongside your medical care, never instead of it.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-the-gp-chronic-condition-management-plan-and-how-does-it-work">What is the GP Chronic Condition Management Plan and how does it work?</h2>
<p>The GP Chronic Condition Management Plan, or GPCCMP, is the plan your GP prepares to help you access Medicare-subsidised allied health services, including dietitian appointments. From 1 July 2025 it replaced the older GP Management Plans and Team Care Arrangements, so one plan now covers what used to take a couple of pieces of paperwork.</p>
<p>Your GP prepares the plan during a consultation and reviews it with you over time, which is a chance to check whether your allied health support is still meeting your needs.</p>
<p>Under the plan you can usually access up to five Medicare-subsidised allied health sessions each calendar year. Those sessions can all go to dietitian visits, or they can be shared with other allied health services such as physiotherapy or podiatry. The exact number of visits that attract a rebate depends on your care plan, so your GP is the person to confirm it (Dietitians Australia).</p>
<h2 id="who-is-eligible-for-medicare-rebates-to-see-a-dietitian">Who is eligible for Medicare rebates to see a dietitian?</h2>
<p>Generally you need a chronic health condition that has lasted, or is expected to last, six months or longer. Your GP uses their clinical judgement to decide whether structured allied health care would help you.</p>
<p>Plain examples of chronic conditions a GP might write a plan for include arthritis, diabetes, kidney disease, heart disease and eating disorders (Dietitians Australia). These are conditions where ongoing nutrition support can be part of a broader management approach.</p>
<p>Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people may be able to access additional allied health sessions through a separate pathway, so it&#8217;s worth asking your GP about that too.</p>
<p>Your GP decides whether you meet the criteria. A dietitian can&#8217;t create the plan for you, so the first step is always a conversation with your GP.</p>
<h2 id="how-does-the-rebate-work-at-your-appointment">How does the rebate work at your appointment?</h2>
<p>A Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan. The rebate covers part of the cost of a session, not all of it, and you pay the gap between the dietitian&#8217;s fee and the rebate.</p>
<p>Dietitians Australia does not set fee guidelines, so the cost of a consultation depends on the type of service and the practice (Dietitians Australia). That means the gap you pay will vary too.</p>
<p>The most reliable way to understand your out-of-pocket cost is to ask the practice when you book. Some practices can process the claim for you on the day; others will ask you to pay the fee and claim the rebate yourself through Medicare.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-you-set-up-a-gpccmp-before-seeing-a-dietitian">How do you set up a GPCCMP before seeing a dietitian?</h2>
<p>Book a longer GP appointment and explain that you would like a plan that includes dietitian support. Your GP will check whether you&#8217;re eligible and prepare the GPCCMP if it&#8217;s appropriate.</p>
<p>The plan must be in place before your dietitian appointment. A Medicare claim can&#8217;t be backdated, so the GPCCMP needs to exist before your first session.</p>
<p>If your nutrition support relates to an eating disorder, there is a separate Eating Disorder Treatment Plan pathway with its own rules. Your GP can talk you through which one fits.</p>
<h2 id="does-private-health-insurance-or-another-fund-cover-dietitian-visits">Does private health insurance or another fund cover dietitian visits?</h2>
<p>Most private health funds cover visits to Accredited Practising Dietitians in private practice, depending on your level of extras cover (Dietitians Australia). What you can claim depends on your policy, so check with your fund.</p>
<p>The Department of Veterans&#8217; Affairs may cover visits to an Accredited Practising Dietitian for Veteran Gold Card holders, or Veteran White Card holders where treatment is for an accepted service-related condition (Dietitians Australia).</p>
<p>NDIS participants may be able to use funding for dietetic services under capacity building and core supports, depending on their plan and goals (Dietitians Australia).</p>
<p>Government workplace injury and transport accident compensation schemes may also cover dietitian visits where your nutrition needs relate to an accepted claim.</p>

<h2 id="what-should-you-check-before-assuming-you-will-get-a-rebate">What should you check before assuming you will get a rebate?</h2>
<p>Confirm your plan is current, and that you want dietitian visits among the allied health sessions you use this year. If you&#8217;ve already used some sessions on another allied health service, you may have fewer left for dietitian visits.</p>
<p>Ask the practice whether the rebate applies to the specific service your appointment attracts, as it may not be the same for every type of dietetic appointment.</p>
<p>Check how the annual limit works alongside any other plan you have, such as an Eating Disorder Treatment Plan or a pathway for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. These have different rules, and the sessions may not simply add together.</p>
