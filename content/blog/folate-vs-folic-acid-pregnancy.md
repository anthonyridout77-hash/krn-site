I get asked this one all the time: should I be taking folate or folic acid? I guess the key thing is this. For most women, folic acid is the evidence-based choice, and Australian guidance recommends 400 micrograms a day from at least one month before conception through the first trimester. Folate from food still matters, but the two aren’t the same thing, and the word "folate" on a label doesn’t always mean what you think it does. Let’s unpack it.

## What is the difference between folate and folic acid?

Folate is the form of vitamin B9 found naturally in food, such as leafy greens, legumes and fruit. Folic acid is the synthetic form. It is more stable than food folate, which is why it is the form used in most supplements and in food fortification.

Methylfolate, also called 5-methyltetrahydrofolate or 5-MTHF, is the active form your body actually uses. Folinic acid is another form that converts readily in the body.

Both terms get used loosely on supplement labels, which is where a lot of the confusion starts. A product labelled "folate" may contain methylfolate, folinic acid or folic acid. Reading the ingredient list, not just the front label, tells you which form you are actually buying.

## How much folate or folic acid do you actually need in pregnancy?

Under the Australian Nutrient Reference Values, women need about 400 micrograms of folate a day, rising to about 600 micrograms a day in pregnancy and 500 micrograms while breastfeeding.

Australian guidance is specific about the supplement. [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/kidsfamilies/MCFhealth/pregnancy/Pages/vitamins-minerals.aspx) recommends a daily supplement with 400 micrograms of folic acid, started at least one month before pregnancy and continued for the first three months. This lines up with national advice from [Pregnancy, Birth and Baby](https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/pregnancy/nutrition-and-lifestyle/folate-and-pregnancy).

Food alone is unlikely to hit the target, which is why a supplement is the standard recommendation. Folate in food is far less concentrated than the dose in a supplement, and cooking and storage reduce it further.

Some women need a different dose. Those with a personal or family history of neural tube defects, certain medical conditions or specific medicines may need a higher amount. That decision should be guided by your GP or specialist, not by a label on a shelf.

## Why does folate matter so much in early pregnancy?

Folate protects against neural tube defects in the baby, including spina bifida, according to [Pregnancy, Birth and Baby](https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/pregnancy/nutrition-and-lifestyle/folate-and-pregnancy). It is one of the most well-established links in pregnancy nutrition, backed by decades of research, and the protective role of vitamin B9 is widely accepted in the literature (see this [2020 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32868164/)).

The neural tube closes very early in pregnancy, often before you even know you are pregnant. That timing is why starting before conception matters so much. If you wait until you see a positive test, the critical window has usually already passed.

We use supplements to supplement your diet, not replace it. The supplement gives you the reliable daily dose, and folate-rich foods bring vitamin B9 along with fibre, iron and the other nutrients a growing baby needs.

## Should you take folic acid or methylfolate?

It depends on your situation, and for most women folic acid remains the form with the strongest evidence. Methylfolate doesn’t need converting, so it is often described as more bioavailable, but better absorption does not automatically mean better pregnancy outcomes. The large trials that showed fewer neural tube defects used folic acid.

Your body converts folic acid to the active form fairly slowly, so at high doses some of it can circulate unmetabolised. High doses of folic acid can also mask a vitamin B12 deficiency, which is one reason more isn’t automatically better.

Research comparing folic acid with 5-MTHF in pregnancy is still developing (see this [2020 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32868164/)), and the clinical evidence on pregnancy outcomes is not yet settled.

MTHFR gene variants often come up in this conversation. Having an MTHFR variant does not automatically mean you need a different form of folate. This is one to talk through with your GP or an Accredited Practising Dietitian, who can look at your whole picture rather than a single gene result.

## What does Australian guidance say about folate in pregnancy?

Australian guidance centres on 400 micrograms of folic acid daily, started at least one month before conception and continued through the first trimester, according to [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/kidsfamilies/MCFhealth/pregnancy/Pages/vitamins-minerals.aspx). It is the consistent recommendation across state and national health advice.

Many women keep taking a pregnancy multivitamin beyond the first trimester, often for the iodine, which is recommended right through pregnancy and breastfeeding. Folic acid itself is only formally recommended for the first trimester.

Australian supplements vary in form and dose, so reading the label matters. The ingredient list will tell you whether the product contains folic acid, methylfolate or folinic acid, and how much.

## Which foods give you the most folate?

Leafy greens, legumes and fruit are the main natural sources of folate. Spinach, kale, lentils, chickpeas, oranges and avocado are all practical options that fit easily into an Australian diet.

Fortified foods are a big source of folate in practice. In Australia, bread-making flour has been fortified with folic acid since 2009, so many women get a background intake from bread without thinking about it.

Folate from food is less concentrated than a supplement, so it supports rather than replaces the daily dose. You would need to eat a large volume of folate-rich foods every single day to reliably reach the target, which isn’t realistic for most of us.

Simple little switches add up over a week. A handful of spinach in a smoothie, a serve of lentils in a curry, or a piece of fruit as a snack all contribute. If your appetite is limited in early pregnancy, the supplement matters even more because food alone is unlikely to meet your needs.

## Are there risks or upper limits with folic acid?

More is not automatically better, and high doses should only be taken under medical guidance. As above, high doses of folic acid can mask a vitamin B12 deficiency, which matters if it goes undetected.

Whether unmetabolised folic acid has any measurable effect on the baby is still being researched, and the evidence is not conclusive.

Your dose should match your situation, not the loudest label on the shelf. Standard pregnancy multivitamins usually contain the recommended 400 micrograms, but some products contain much more, and it is easy to double up if you take more than one product.

Bring your supplements to your GP or dietitian appointment so they can be reviewed together. Seeing the actual product, including the form of folate and the dose, makes for a much more useful conversation than trying to remember it.

## Key Takeaways

* Folate is the natural form of vitamin B9 in food; folic acid is the synthetic form used in supplements and fortification.
* Australian guidance recommends 400 micrograms of folic acid daily, starting at least one month before pregnancy and continuing through the first trimester.
* Folate needs rise to about 600 micrograms a day in pregnancy and 500 micrograms while breastfeeding.
* Folate protects against neural tube defects such as spina bifida, and the neural tube closes before many pregnancies are confirmed.
* Methylfolate is often described as more bioavailable, but better absorption does not automatically mean better pregnancy outcomes.
* High doses of folic acid can circulate unmetabolised and may mask a vitamin B12 deficiency.
* Some women need a different dose, including those with a personal or family history of neural tube defects, guided by their GP or specialist.
