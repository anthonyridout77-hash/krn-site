If you have been told you need the glucose test in this pregnancy, and you have a friend or a sister who had it a couple of years ago, you may find the numbers do not match. That is not a mistake at the lab. Australia changed the criteria.

## What actually changed

In June 2025 the Australasian Diabetes in Pregnancy Society published new consensus recommendations for how gestational diabetes is screened for, diagnosed and classified. They replace criteria that had stood since 2014. The headline change is that the diagnostic glucose thresholds went up, which means some women who would have been diagnosed under the old numbers will not be under the new ones.

Health services have been working through the change at different speeds. Victorian pathology services moved across on 4 August 2025, and health services elsewhere have been asked to review the new criteria and update their local guidelines. So what appears on your report depends partly on where you are being cared for, and it is a fair thing to ask your midwife or GP about.

## The new numbers

The test itself has not changed. It is still a 75 gram oral glucose tolerance test, with blood taken fasting, at one hour and at two hours. What changed is the level at which each of those three readings counts.

Under the 2025 recommendations, gestational diabetes is diagnosed if any one of these applies: a fasting plasma glucose of 5.3 to 6.9 mmol/L, a one hour reading of 10.6 mmol/L or above, or a two hour reading of 9.0 to 11.0 mmol/L.

The previous thresholds were 5.1 fasting, 10.0 at one hour and 8.5 at two hours, so all three have moved up. The fasting and two hour figures are written as ranges rather than as a single cut off because readings above the top of each range point to diabetes in pregnancy rather than gestational diabetes. That is a different classification and a different conversation with your doctor.

## Testing before 20 weeks, and the new name for it

The recommendations also formalise something that was previously done inconsistently. Women with risk factors are advised to be tested before 20 weeks, ideally between ten and fourteen weeks. If gestational diabetes is found in that early window it now has its own label: early gestational diabetes mellitus.

Everyone else, unless diabetes has already been detected in the current pregnancy, is advised to have the 75 gram two hour test at 24 to 28 weeks. That part has not changed.

## What this means if you are pregnant right now

Three practical things.

First, if you were diagnosed earlier in this pregnancy under the old criteria, your diagnosis stands. Criteria changing does not retrospectively undiagnose anybody, and the care you have had was appropriate for the numbers in front of your team at the time.

Second, if you are comparing your result with someone else’s, check which year they were tested. A fasting reading of 5.2 meant one thing in 2023 and means something different now.

Third, if your result sits close to a threshold in either direction, that is worth a proper conversation rather than a number read off a page. Your own risk picture, your history and what the rest of the test showed all belong in that conversation.

## Where nutrition fits, and where it does not

Nutrition is not the whole of gestational diabetes care and it does not replace the medical side of it. What it is, is the part most women are handed with the least explanation.

The general pattern that comes up again and again in the research is not a list of forbidden foods. It is about what arrives alongside the carbohydrate. Carbohydrate eaten with protein, fibre and a little fat behaves differently from carbohydrate eaten on its own, and spreading it across the day tends to sit better than concentrating it into one or two large meals.

What that looks like in practice is smaller and duller than most people expect. Porridge with yoghurt and nuts rather than toast and jam on its own. A tin of beans going into the pasta sauce. A boiled egg next to the fruit. None of it is dramatic, and that is rather the point, because a pattern only helps if it survives a real week.

## When it is worth seeing a dietitian

Not everyone needs one. It tends to be most useful when the numbers are not doing what you expected despite reasonable effort, when there is something else going on at the same time such as nausea, strong food aversions, a restricted diet or a previous pregnancy with gestational diabetes, or simply when you have been handed a set of rules and nobody has explained how to fit them around your actual life.

A referral can often go through your GP under a chronic disease management plan, and it is a completely ordinary thing to ask about.
