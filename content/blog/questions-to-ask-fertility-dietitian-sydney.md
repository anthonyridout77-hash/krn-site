If you’re in Sydney and trying to conceive, you might be scrolling through dietitian websites and wondering how on earth to pick one. I guess the key thing is to ask a few good questions before you book, because the right dietitian for you depends on your situation, the way you like to work and what’s going on with your cycle or treatment. You also don’t need to be in the same city. I’m in Brisbane and I work with women and couples all over Australia by telehealth. Whoever you choose, a fertility dietitian supports your GP and fertility clinic, and no one can promise a pregnancy.

## Are you an Accredited Practising Dietitian?

This is the first question to ask. The title “nutritionist” isn’t regulated in Australia, so anyone can use it. An Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) has completed accredited university training in dietetics, keeps up ongoing professional development and follows evidence-based practice standards. You can check anyone’s APD status through [Dietitians Australia](https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/find-an-apd) in a minute or two.

## How much of your work is fertility nutrition?

Fertility nutrition covers a lot of ground: preconception, PMOS (previously called PCOS), endometriosis, thyroid conditions, IVF preparation and male fertility. Someone who works in this area regularly will understand cycle timing, the language your fertility clinic uses and the practical reality of trying to conceive month after month.

If you have a particular condition, ask about it by name. For PMOS, for example, [Jean Hailes](https://www.jeanhailes.org.au/health-a-z/pcos) has good background on why eating pattern and lifestyle sit at the centre of care.

## How do you use the evidence?

A good answer sounds something like “it depends”. Most of the research on diet and fertility is observational, and it points to overall eating patterns rather than single hero foods. Mediterranean-style eating, with plenty of vegetables, wholegrains, legumes, fish and olive oil, has the most consistent support, as summarised in a review by [Gaskins and Chavarro](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajog.2017.10.003).

Be wary of anyone promising a pregnancy, selling an expensive protocol up front, or using fear to get you to buy supplements. Supplements are individual. Ask how they decide what you need and when they’ll review it.

## How is IVF support different from trying naturally?

The foundations are the same, but the timing and focus change. Preconception nutrition is about your eating pattern over months, not a short burst in the weeks before a cycle. With IVF there’s extra planning around egg collection, medication schedules, appetite changes and keeping things steady through a demanding few weeks.

Ask whether the dietitian is comfortable working around your clinic’s protocol, and how they’ll fit in with your specialist’s plan rather than adding a competing one.

## Do you work with partners too?

Fertility is a team sport. Alcohol, smoking, sleep, stress, activity and overall eating pattern can all play a part in sperm health, and [Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/infertility) notes that lifestyle changes can make a difference for both partners. Sperm take around three months to develop, so changes are worth starting well before a planned cycle.

Ask whether partners can join the consults. Working on it together shares the mental load and makes the changes far easier to keep up at home.

## Will you talk to my GP and fertility clinic?

Nutrition supports medical and fertility clinic care and never replaces it. A dietitian can’t order blood tests, so it helps when they write down what to ask your GP for and write back to whoever referred you. Ask what they’ll send and to whom.

It’s also worth asking about Medicare. You don’t need a referral to see a dietitian, but a Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan, so check with your GP.

## Can we work together by telehealth?

Fertility nutrition suits telehealth really well, because the work is mostly about how you eat day to day, supplement planning and practical goals, none of which need you in a room. It also means your choice isn’t limited to whoever has rooms near you in Sydney. Ask how follow-ups work and whether you can get in touch between appointments.

## Key Takeaways

* Check for the APD credential through Dietitians Australia before you book.
* Ask how much of their work is fertility nutrition, and about your condition by name.
* Look for a pattern-based, evidence-based approach and steer clear of promised outcomes.
* IVF support adds timing and medication planning to the same foundations.
* Partners matter too, and changes are worth starting about three months ahead.
* Telehealth means you can work with a fertility dietitian anywhere in Australia.
* No referral is needed, and a Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan.
