It is one of the most common things women raise in an appointment, and it is almost always raised last, quickly, and with an apology. Constipation in pregnancy is very common, it is usually not dangerous, and it is one of the few pregnancy complaints where a small number of ordinary, boring changes are genuinely worth trying before anything else.

By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed November 2026.

This article is general education rather than individual advice. If you are pregnant and uncomfortable, your GP, midwife or obstetrician is the right first stop, and nothing here replaces that conversation.

## What the evidence actually supports

Less than most people expect, and it is worth being honest about that.

The Cochrane review of interventions for treating constipation in pregnancy (Rungsiprakarn and colleagues, 2015) searched for randomised trials of both drug and non-drug treatments. It found four studies, and only two of them, covering 180 women in total, produced usable data. No meta-analysis was possible.

Of those two, one compared fibre supplementation with no intervention in 40 women. The review's conclusion was that "fibre supplementation may increase frequency of stools compared with no intervention (moderate quality evidence)", at around 2.24 more bowel movements per week.

The other compared stimulant with bulk-forming laxatives in 140 women. The review found that "stimulant laxatives appear to be more effective in improvement of constipation (moderate quality evidence)", while also causing more diarrhoea and more abdominal discomfort.

The authors' overall position was that "there is insufficient evidence to comprehensively assess the effectiveness and safety of interventions" in this area.

Two things follow from that. The first is that fibre is the one dietary change with a measured effect, so starting there is reasonable. The second is that the effect is modest rather than dramatic, and that anything beyond food belongs in a conversation with your GP, midwife or pharmacist rather than in a blog post.

## The fibre target goes up in pregnancy, and most plates do not

This is the part that surprises people. In the Australian and New Zealand Nutrient Reference Values, published by the NHMRC, the adequate intake for dietary fibre is 25 grams a day for adult women. In pregnancy it rises to 28 grams a day for women aged 19 to 50. In breastfeeding it rises again, to 30 grams a day for the same age group.

So pregnancy adds roughly three grams a day, and breastfeeding adds about five. On paper that is a small number. In practice almost nobody adds it, because pregnancy is usually the moment when eating gets harder rather than easier. Nausea narrows what appeals. Reflux rules things out. Appetite moves around. The foods that quietly carry most of the fibre in an Australian diet, which are wholegrains, legumes, fruit, vegetables and nuts, are often the first ones to drop away.

Three grams is about one piece of fruit with the skin on. It is about half a cup of cooked lentils stirred through something you were already making. It is the difference between white and wholegrain bread across a day. The target is reachable. It just has to be noticed.

## In practice, fibre on its own is rarely the answer

Here is the pattern I see most often in clinic. Someone reads that fibre helps, adds a large amount of it quickly, does not change anything else, and feels worse rather than better.

Fibre needs fluid to do its job, and it needs to arrive gradually rather than all at once. So when I am working through this with someone, I am looking at fibre and fluid together, and at how fast the change is happening, not at fibre alone. That is a clinical judgement rather than a trial finding, and I would rather label it as such.

## Where iron supplements fit

If your symptoms started around the same time as a new supplement, that timing is worth mentioning.

Constipation is one of the side effects the Cochrane review of daily oral iron supplementation in pregnancy formally tracks as an outcome, alongside nausea, heartburn and vomiting. That does not mean iron is the cause in any individual case, and it is not a reason to stop a supplement that has been prescribed to you. It does mean the question is a fair one to put to your GP or midwife, who can look at your iron studies and at the dose, form and timing, and decide whether anything should change.

## Four questions to ask before you reach for a supplement

These are the four I work through first, and they are worth bringing to your own appointment.

1. How much fibre am I actually getting, and which foods is it coming from? Most people are closer than they fear, or much further away than they think, and guessing is the problem.
2. How much fluid am I drinking across a whole day, rather than just at meals?
3. Did this start when something else changed? A new supplement, a new medication, a change in how much you are moving, or a stretch of poor appetite are all worth noticing.
4. If food is not enough on its own, which option actually suits pregnancy, at what dose, and for how long? That is a question for your GP, midwife or pharmacist.

## When a dietitian is worth it

Not everyone needs one for this. Plenty of people close a three gram gap on their own once they can see where it is.

Where an Accredited Practising Dietitian earns their place is when it is not simple. When nausea or food aversions rule out most of the usual high fibre foods. When reflux makes large meals impossible. When you are also managing low iron, or gestational diabetes, and a change in one direction has to not undo something else. When you have already tried adding fibre and it made things worse, which usually means the how rather than the what was the problem.

That is the work: finding the actual intake, finding the gap, and closing it in a way that fits the pregnancy you are having rather than an ideal one.
