## Who I am and why this matters

I have been a dietitian for more than 20 years, and I am a mum of three. Gestational diabetes is one of the most common reasons pregnant women come to see me. Most arrive a little rattled. They have a glucose meter, a sheet of rules and a lot of questions.

This article walks you through what actually happens when you see me, step by step. If you were only diagnosed in the last day or two, start with [what to do when you have just been diagnosed](/blogs/gestational-diabetes-just-diagnosed-what-now/) and come back here after.

## What to look for in a gestational diabetes dietitian

Whoever you see, these are the things I would check.

- **The APD credential.** Accredited Practising Dietitians are listed on the Dietitians Australia find an APD directory.
- **Regular pregnancy work.** Ask how much of their week is spent on gestational diabetes.
- **They follow your team's targets.** Your blood glucose targets come from your doctor or clinic. A dietitian works within them.
- **No extreme carbohydrate cutting.** You and your baby still need carbohydrate.
- **They read your actual numbers.** A good plan changes when your readings change.
- **Clear information about costs before you book.**

If your Brisbane hospital or clinic already offers you a dietitian, please use that service. A private dietitian can add more time and more detail, especially if you have a private obstetrician, a restricted diet, shift work or a busy household.

## What to expect at your first consult

Your first consult is 75 minutes. Before we meet, I ask you to send or bring:

- your glucose tolerance test result
- your blood glucose readings so far, even if there are only a few days
- the targets your team has given you
- your medications and supplements
- a rough idea of a normal day of eating

We start with your story. How you were diagnosed, how far along you are, and how you are feeling about it. Then we look at your readings together and find the patterns. A high fasting number needs a different conversation from a high reading after lunch.

From there we build your plan. That usually covers how to spread carbohydrate across the day, which carbohydrates suit your readings, what to pair them with, and breakfasts you can face. Breakfast is often the tricky meal. My [gestational diabetes meal plan guide](/blogs/gestational-diabetes-meal-plan-guide/) shows what a typical day can look like.

We also check you are eating enough for pregnancy. Gestational diabetes is not a reason to go hungry.

## What you get after the consult

Within 2 business days you receive a written plan and an individualised supplement plan. The plan is built around your foods, your culture and your week, not a generic handout.

The supplement plan looks at what you already take and what your pregnancy needs, so you are not guessing or doubling up. The NHMRC recommends 150 micrograms of iodine a day in pregnancy, and folic acid in early pregnancy. If you have a thyroid condition, check with your doctor before taking iodine.

## Follow-ups through the rest of your pregnancy

Gestational diabetes changes as your pregnancy goes on. Hormones rise in the last trimester, and readings that were easy at 28 weeks can be harder at 34 weeks.

Follow-ups are short and practical. We look at your latest readings, adjust what is not working and plan for what is coming. That might be a fasting number creeping up, a new medication, or a hospital stay you want snacks for.

Some women need metformin or insulin as well as food changes. That is not failure. Medication decisions always sit with your doctor, and I adjust your food plan to fit.

## Working with your Brisbane care team

Your obstetrician, midwife, GP, hospital clinic or diabetes educator stays in charge of your medical care. With your permission, I can send a summary of your plan to your GP so everyone is on the same page.

Please contact your doctor, midwife or hospital straight away if you feel unwell, if your readings are outside the range they asked you to report, or if you are worried about your baby's movements. Those are never questions to save for a dietitian.

## About the test numbers

The Australasian Diabetes in Pregnancy Society updated the diagnostic criteria in 2025. Under the new criteria, gestational diabetes is diagnosed if any one of these applies on the 75 gram glucose tolerance test: a fasting plasma glucose of 5.3 to 6.9 mmol/L, a one hour reading of 10.6 mmol/L or above, or a two hour reading of 9.0 to 11.0 mmol/L.

Services have moved across at different speeds, so ask your team which criteria they use. I explain the change in [why the gestational diabetes test numbers changed](/blogs/gestational-diabetes-test-numbers-changed-adips-2025/).

## When to book

Soon after diagnosis is usually most helpful, because there are often only a few months to work with. It is also worth booking if:

- your readings are hard to predict
- you feel hungry all the time, or scared of food
- you have started metformin or insulin
- you had gestational diabetes in a past pregnancy and want a plan early this time

## Frequently asked questions

### How soon after diagnosis should I see a dietitian?

As soon as you can. Most women are diagnosed between 24 and 28 weeks, so there is not long to settle into a routine. Even one consult can make the weeks ahead feel more manageable.

### Do I need a referral?

No referral is needed to book privately. If you want to explore a Medicare rebate, talk to your GP first. A rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan, and the GP decides eligibility.

### Do I have to stop eating carbs?

No. Carbohydrate is an important part of pregnancy eating. We work on the type, the amount and how it is spread across your day.

### Can I see you by telehealth if I live outside Brisbane?

Yes. I see women across Australia by telehealth video. It covers the same ground as an in-person consult in Milton.

### How many appointments will I need?

It depends on your readings and how far along you are. Some women need one consult and a check-in. Others like regular follow-ups until birth. We decide together.

### What happens after my baby is born?

For most women glucose levels return to normal after birth. You will usually be asked to have a repeat glucose test, and your risk of type 2 diabetes later in life is higher, so keep up check-ups with your GP.
