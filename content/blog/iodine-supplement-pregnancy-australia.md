Short answer: yes. If you’re trying for a baby, pregnant or breastfeeding in Australia, the advice is a daily supplement with 150 micrograms of iodine, because most of us simply don’t get enough from food during these stages. I guess the key thing is that this is one of the few supplements where the evidence is really clear, and it’s one small habit that does a big job for your baby’s brain. If you have a thyroid condition, have a chat with your GP before you start.

*By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed October 2026.*

## Why is iodine so important during pregnancy and breastfeeding?

Your thyroid uses iodine to make thyroid hormones, and in pregnancy it makes about 50% more of them, according to [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/kidsfamilies/MCFhealth/maternity/Pages/iodine-supplements-factsheet.aspx). That extra demand is why your iodine needs jump so much when you’re expecting.

Those hormones are what your baby relies on for normal growth and for the brain and nervous system to develop. Even mild iodine deficiency in pregnancy and breastfeeding has been linked with effects on a child’s learning and development, which is why the [National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC)](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/about-us/publications/iodine-supplementation-pregnant-and-breastfeeding-women) recommends a supplement for every woman in these stages. That can sound a bit scary, but the practical message is reassuring: a small daily habit looks after a really important window.

Once your baby arrives, breastmilk is their only source of iodine while they’re exclusively breastfed. So your iodine status keeps mattering well after the birth.

## How much iodine do you need when you are pregnant or breastfeeding?

The [NHMRC](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/about-us/publications/iodine-supplementation-pregnant-and-breastfeeding-women) recommends that all women who are pregnant, breastfeeding or considering pregnancy take a daily supplement of 150 micrograms of iodine. [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/kidsfamilies/MCFhealth/maternity/Pages/iodine-supplements-factsheet.aspx) gives the same advice.

Your total daily target from food and supplement together is 220 micrograms in pregnancy and 270 micrograms while breastfeeding, under the Australian Nutrient Reference Values set by the NHMRC. The supplement sits alongside your food, not instead of it.

More isn’t better here. [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/kidsfamilies/MCFhealth/maternity/Pages/iodine-supplements-factsheet.aspx) is clear that there’s no benefit in taking more than 150 micrograms from a supplement. As I often say, sometimes in supplementation there is too much of a good thing.

## Are Australian women actually getting enough iodine from food?

Mostly, no. Pregnant and breastfeeding women in Australia aren’t getting enough iodine through diet alone, according to [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/kidsfamilies/MCFhealth/maternity/Pages/iodine-supplements-factsheet.aspx). That’s the whole reason the supplement advice exists, and it applies even if you eat a really good, varied diet.

Since 2009, bread made in Australia has had to use iodised salt (organic bread is the exception). That lifted iodine levels for the general population, but it doesn’t stretch far enough to cover the higher needs of pregnancy and breastfeeding.

A Tasmanian study of 255 pregnant women, published in [Nutrients (PubMed Central)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6356319/), found a median urinary iodine concentration of 133 micrograms per litre, below the 150 to 249 range considered adequate in pregnancy. So the gap is real and measurable, not just theory.

## Which foods in Australia contain iodine?

The main sources in Australia are bread, eggs, dairy, iodised salt and seafood, according to [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/kidsfamilies/MCFhealth/maternity/Pages/iodine-supplements-factsheet.aspx). They’re all worth including regularly, and they bring plenty of other nutrients along with the iodine.

A couple of things to watch. Organic bread doesn’t have to use iodised salt, and neither do gluten-free products, so if you’re going gluten-free or buying organic loaves you’ll need to be a bit more intentional about iodine from other foods. Fish is a lovely source too; in pregnancy it’s the bigger fish like shark (flake), swordfish and orange roughy that need limiting because of mercury, so lean on lower-mercury choices like salmon, sardines and tinned tuna.

Food always comes first. I guess the key thing is that we use supplements to supplement your diet, not replace it. The evidence just shows food on its own usually falls short for iodine in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

## When should you start taking an iodine supplement?

Ideally before you conceive. In the Tasmanian study published in [Nutrients (PubMed Central)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6356319/), women who started a 150 microgram supplement before conception had a median urinary iodine concentration of 196 micrograms per litre, compared with 137.5 for women who started after their pregnancy was confirmed.

The same study found women taking an iodine-containing supplement had a median of 155, compared with 120 for those who never took one and 90 for those who had stopped. So timing matters, and so does sticking with it.

It takes about three months to build your eggs, which is why I like to look at supplements as part of that preconception runway. If you’re planning, it’s a no-brainer to check your label now. For the other half of that conversation, have a read of [folate or folic acid in pregnancy](https://www.katridout.com.au/blogs/folate-vs-folic-acid-pregnancy/) and [what to eat in the 3 months before trying to conceive](https://www.katridout.com.au/blogs/what-to-eat-3-months-before-trying-to-conceive/).

## Are there any risks or times to be careful with iodine?

If you have a thyroid condition, talk to your doctor before taking an iodine supplement. Both the [NHMRC](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/about-us/publications/iodine-supplementation-pregnant-and-breastfeeding-women) and [NSW Health](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/kidsfamilies/MCFhealth/maternity/Pages/iodine-supplements-factsheet.aspx) say this, because some thyroid conditions change how your body handles iodine. Always go on your specialist here.

Skip kelp and seaweed supplements in pregnancy and breastfeeding. The amount of iodine in them varies a lot and they can carry heavy metals, so they’re not a safe swap for a measured supplement.

And stick to the recommended dose. Stacking a pregnancy multi with a separate iodine tablet can quietly push you over, so it’s worth adding up what’s on every label.

## How do you choose an iodine supplement in Australia?

Don’t assume your pregnancy multivitamin has it covered. Products on the shelf vary, and some prenatal supplements contain little or no iodine. Turn the box over and check that iodine is listed and that it gives you around 150 micrograms a day.

If you’re taking more than one product, look at the back and the front of every one so you’re not doubling up. And leave the kelp-based products on the shelf.

## Key Takeaways

* The NHMRC recommends a daily 150 microgram iodine supplement when planning pregnancy, during pregnancy and while breastfeeding.
* Total daily targets are 220 micrograms in pregnancy and 270 micrograms while breastfeeding, from food and supplement together.
* There’s no benefit in taking more than 150 micrograms from a supplement.
* Pregnant and breastfeeding women in Australia usually don’t meet iodine needs from food alone, even with iodised salt in bread.
* Organic and gluten-free breads don’t have to use iodised salt.
* Starting before conception is linked with better iodine levels than starting once pregnancy is confirmed.
* Avoid kelp and seaweed supplements, and check with your doctor first if you have a thyroid condition.
