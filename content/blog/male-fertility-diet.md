When couples have difficulty conceiving, attention often focuses on the woman. But male factor infertility contributes to approximately 40–50% of all cases — and nutrition plays a significant role in sperm health that’s frequently overlooked.

As a fertility dietitian, I work with both partners. Here’s what the research says about how diet affects male fertility — and what men can do about it.

## Why Male Nutrition Matters for Fertility

Sperm take approximately 72–90 days to develop (spermatogenesis). During this window, nutritional status directly influences:

* **Sperm concentration** — the number of sperm per millilitre
* **Sperm motility** — the ability of sperm to swim effectively
* **Sperm morphology** — the shape and structure of sperm
* **DNA fragmentation** — the integrity of the genetic material sperm carry

High sperm DNA fragmentation in particular is associated with reduced fertilisation rates, lower embryo quality, and higher miscarriage rates — even when other semen parameters appear normal. Antioxidant nutrition is one of the most evidence-supported strategies for reducing DNA fragmentation.

## The Antioxidant Imperative

Sperm are exceptionally vulnerable to oxidative stress. Unlike most cells, mature sperm have very limited antioxidant defences of their own, making dietary antioxidant intake critical. Oxidative stress damages sperm membranes, impairs motility, and — most significantly — causes DNA strand breaks.

Key antioxidants for male fertility:

* **Zinc** — Essential for testosterone production, sperm maturation, and DNA synthesis. Australia’s most commonly under-consumed fertility mineral in men. Found in oysters, red meat, pumpkin seeds, and legumes.
* **Selenium** — Required for a specific antioxidant enzyme (GPx5) that protects sperm during epididymal transit. Found in Brazil nuts (just 2 per day provides adequate selenium), fish, and eggs.
* **Vitamin C** — Water-soluble antioxidant abundant in seminal fluid. Shown to reduce DNA fragmentation in multiple studies. Found in citrus, capsicum, broccoli, and kiwi.
* **Vitamin E** — Fat-soluble antioxidant that protects sperm cell membranes. Often works synergistically with vitamin C. Found in nuts, seeds, and avocado.
* **Lycopene** — The carotenoid that gives tomatoes their red colour; consistently associated with improved sperm concentration and motility. Best absorbed from cooked tomatoes with a fat source (e.g. tomato paste in olive oil).
* **Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)** — Mitochondrial antioxidant essential for sperm motility (flagellar energy production). Multiple RCTs show improvements in motility and concentration with 200–300mg/day supplementation.

## Omega-3 Fatty Acids and Sperm Structure

DHA (docosahexaenoic acid) is the dominant fatty acid in the sperm head, where it’s essential for membrane fluidity and the acrosome reaction (required for egg penetration). Men with poor sperm morphology tend to have lower DHA levels. Regular consumption of oily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel) or a quality fish oil supplement (1–2g DHA+EPA daily) is recommended.

## Foods and Habits That Harm Sperm Quality

Just as important as what to eat is what to reduce or avoid:

* **Ultra-processed foods** — High in trans fats and refined carbohydrates, associated with lower sperm concentration and motility
* **Processed meats** — Bacon, salami, and deli meats are associated with reduced sperm morphology in multiple studies
* **Alcohol** — Even moderate intake (>7 standard drinks/week) is associated with reduced testosterone and sperm parameters
* **Excessive soy** — High isoflavone intake may have mild oestrogenic effects; occasional consumption is fine but daily large quantities aren’t ideal while trying to conceive
* **Heat** — Scrotal temperature is critical for spermatogenesis; hot tubs, saunas, and tight underwear are associated with reduced sperm production

## The Male Fertility Diet: A Practical Summary

Rather than obsessing over individual foods, aim for an overall dietary pattern that:

* Is rich in colourful vegetables and fruit (antioxidants)
* Includes oily fish 2–3 times per week (DHA, omega-3s)
* Contains adequate lean protein (zinc, selenium)
* Uses olive oil as the primary fat (anti-inflammatory)
* Limits ultra-processed foods, alcohol, and processed meats

This is essentially a Mediterranean-style eating pattern — the same pattern shown to benefit female fertility — making it straightforward for couples to adopt together.

## Should Men Take Fertility Supplements?

The short answer is yes — but the right ones, at the right doses, for long enough to have effect. Generic multivitamins are rarely sufficient. A fertility dietitian can assess your diet, review any relevant semen analysis results, and recommend a targeted supplement stack based on where your gaps are.

Common evidence-based supplements for male fertility include CoQ10, zinc, selenium, vitamin E, vitamin C, omega-3s, and folate. Most require at least 90 days of consistent use before showing measurable improvement in semen parameters.

## When to See a Fertility Dietitian

If you’ve received a semen analysis showing any abnormality — low count, poor motility, high DNA fragmentation, or abnormal morphology — a fertility dietitian can help you understand the nutritional factors that may be contributing and develop a targeted plan. Nutrition alone won’t overcome severe male factor infertility, but in many cases of mild-to-moderate impairment, it makes a clinically meaningful difference to outcomes.
