If your feed looks anything like mine lately, protein is everywhere. Protein coffee, protein water, protein pasta, creators proudly tracking 150 grams a day. The trend even has a name this year, “proteinmaxxing”, and I’ve had several pregnant clients quietly ask me whether they should be doing it too.

So let’s talk about it. Because protein really does matter in pregnancy, probably more than most people realise. It’s just that what your body needs while growing a baby has very little to do with the version you’re seeing online.

## Why protein matters when you’re pregnant

Protein provides the building blocks for your baby’s tissues and organs, the placenta, your expanding blood volume and the changes happening in your own body. Your needs rise as pregnancy progresses, mostly through the second and third trimesters.

In Australia, the recommended intake sits around 60 grams a day in the second and third trimesters, up from roughly 46 grams a day before pregnancy. That’s a meaningful increase, but notice what it isn’t. It isn’t 120 grams. It isn’t a shake with every meal. For most women it’s an extra snack or a slightly bigger serve at dinner.

A breakfast with eggs and dairy is an easy way to get a head start on your protein for the day.

## What 60 grams actually looks like

Here’s a fairly ordinary day that gets there without a single supplement:

Two eggs on wholegrain toast for breakfast gives you around 15 grams. A small tub of Greek yoghurt with a handful of nuts mid-morning adds about 15 more. Cheese and crackers in the afternoon, another 7 or so. A palm-sized piece of chicken, fish or a generous serve of lentils at dinner brings 25 to 30. A glass of milk before bed tops it off with 9.

That’s a normal day of food. No powders, no tracking apps, no protein water.

## The first trimester is a different story

Here’s the part the trend never mentions: in early pregnancy, protein foods are often exactly what you can’t face. Meat, eggs and fish are some of the most common aversions I see in clinic, usually right when nausea is at its worst.

A few things that help my clients. Cold protein foods have far less smell than hot ones, so yoghurt, cheese with crackers, cold chicken or a milky smoothie often go down easier than a cooked dinner. Baked beans on toast is a quietly brilliant option. And warm, mild things like a blended lentil and vegetable soup can be comforting on a cold July evening without being confronting.

If some weeks are mostly crackers and toast, please don’t panic. Your body is clever, it draws on stores, and appetite usually returns. There is plenty of time to catch up.

## Do you need a protein powder?

Usually, food gets you there first, and it brings iron, iodine, choline and omega-3s along for the ride, which a scoop of powder mostly doesn’t. Some powders also contain added herbs or high-dose vitamins that aren’t designed with pregnancy in mind, so if you’re considering one, it’s worth checking your specific product with your dietitian, GP or midwife rather than grabbing what the algorithm suggests.

## When it’s worth getting individual support

Some situations really do change the picture: vegetarian or vegan eating, twins, gestational diabetes, ongoing nausea or a history of low iron. If any of those are you, tailored advice can take a lot of the guesswork (and the guilt) out of it.
