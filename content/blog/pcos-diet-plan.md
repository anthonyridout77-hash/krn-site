Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common hormonal disorder in women of reproductive age, affecting approximately 1 in 8 Australian women. Despite how common it is, the dietary advice around PCOS is often contradictory, overly restrictive, or based on outdated science.

As a dietitian specialising in PCOS and fertility nutrition, this is one of the conditions I treat most frequently. Here’s what the current evidence actually says about nutrition for PCOS — and how to eat in a way that works with your body, not against it.

## Why Diet Matters So Much in PCOS

PCOS is fundamentally a condition of hormonal imbalance — elevated androgens (like testosterone), irregular or absent ovulation, and often (though not always) polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound. For the majority of women with PCOS, **insulin resistance** sits at the centre of this dysfunction.

When cells become resistant to insulin, the pancreas produces more of it to compensate. High insulin levels then signal the ovaries to produce more androgens, which suppress ovulation and drive the symptoms most women with PCOS recognise: irregular periods, acne, excess hair growth, and difficulty conceiving. Addressing insulin resistance through diet is therefore one of the most powerful things you can do for PCOS — and the good news is that relatively modest improvements in insulin sensitivity can have a significant impact on symptoms and fertility.

## The Core Principles of a PCOS Diet

### 1. Prioritise low-glycaemic carbohydrates

This doesn’t mean eliminating carbohydrates — it means choosing carbohydrates that raise blood glucose gradually rather than sharply. High-GI foods (white bread, sugary drinks, refined cereals, white rice) cause rapid glucose spikes followed by high insulin output, which worsens insulin resistance over time.

Instead, focus on: whole grains (oats, barley, brown rice, quinoa), legumes (lentils, chickpeas, kidney beans), starchy vegetables (sweet potato, corn), and whole fruit rather than fruit juice.

### 2. Eat protein at every meal

Protein slows gastric emptying, blunts post-meal glucose rise, and supports satiety — all important for insulin management. It also supports muscle mass, which is the body’s primary site of glucose disposal. Aim for 25–30g of protein at breakfast specifically, as research shows morning protein intake has particular benefits for blood glucose regulation throughout the day.

Good protein sources: eggs, Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese, legumes, chicken, fish, tofu, tempeh.

### 3. Don’t fear fat — choose the right kinds

Dietary fat doesn’t raise blood glucose and is a critical component of hormone production. Women with PCOS often benefit from a higher-fat, lower-refined-carbohydrate eating pattern. Prioritise monounsaturated fats (olive oil, avocado, nuts) and omega-3 fatty acids (oily fish, walnuts, flaxseed). Limit saturated fat from ultra-processed foods, and minimise trans fats entirely.

### 4. Anti-inflammatory eating

Women with PCOS have chronically elevated inflammatory markers. An anti-inflammatory dietary pattern — rich in colourful vegetables, berries, oily fish, olive oil, herbs, and spices — helps reduce this systemic inflammation, which in turn improves insulin sensitivity and reduces androgen production.

### 5. Adequate fibre every day

Dietary fibre slows glucose absorption, feeds beneficial gut bacteria (gut microbiome diversity is impaired in PCOS), and reduces androgens by binding to them in the gut and promoting their excretion. Aim for 25–38g fibre daily from vegetables, whole grains, legumes, and fruit. Most Australian women eat about half this amount.

## Key Supplements for PCOS

### Myo-inositol

Inositol is one of the more widely researched supplements in PCOS, usually studied as a myo-inositol to D-chiro-inositol ratio of 40:1. The research has examined its role in insulin signalling, androgen levels and menstrual cycle regularity. Whether it suits you, at what dose, and alongside what else, is a clinical decision, so raise it with your GP or specialist and we can work through it together in a consult.

### Magnesium

Insulin resistance is associated with lower cellular magnesium, and magnesium supplementation has been shown to improve insulin sensitivity. Many women with PCOS are also subclinically deficient. Found in leafy greens, nuts, seeds, and dark chocolate.

### Vitamin D

Vitamin D receptors are present in ovarian tissue and are involved in insulin signalling. Low vitamin D is extremely common in PCOS and associated with worse outcomes. Have your level checked — many women with PCOS need therapeutic supplementation to reach optimal levels (75–100 nmol/L).

### Omega-3s

Regular fish oil supplementation (2–4g DHA+EPA daily) reduces inflammatory markers, lowers triglycerides (commonly elevated in PCOS), and modestly reduces testosterone levels in some studies.

## What About Weight and PCOS?

Not all women with PCOS are overweight — approximately 20–30% of women with PCOS are lean. For those who are, even a 5–10% reduction in body weight can meaningfully improve insulin sensitivity, restore ovulation, and reduce androgen levels. However, restrictive dieting often backfires in PCOS by triggering cortisol responses that worsen insulin resistance.

A sustainable, nourishing eating pattern based on the principles above — without a focus on restriction or calorie counting — will typically produce gradual improvements in body composition alongside hormone balance. The goal is metabolic health, not a number on a scale.

## Foods to Limit with PCOS

* **Sugar and refined carbohydrates** — soft drinks, lollies, white bread, pastries, most breakfast cereals
* **Ultra-processed foods** — packaged snacks, fast food, commercial baked goods
* **Alcohol** — metabolised similarly to sugar, worsens insulin resistance, and disrupts hormonal signalling
* **Excess caffeine** — can elevate cortisol, particularly problematic when cortisol is already dysregulated in PCOS

## The PCOS Nutrition Timeline: What to Expect

Dietary changes for PCOS typically take 3–6 months to show meaningful impact on hormonal markers and menstrual regularity. This is because hormonal feedback loops change slowly, and ovarian function responds over multiple cycles. Blood glucose improvements often occur within weeks, but don’t expect the full benefit until at least 3 months of consistent changes.
