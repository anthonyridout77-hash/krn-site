<h2 id="what-should-i-look-for-in-a-fertility-dietitian-in-brisbane">What should I look for in a fertility dietitian in Brisbane?</h2>
<p>The first thing to check is whether the person is an Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD). In Australia, an APD has completed an accredited university qualification, meets ongoing professional development requirements and is held to professional standards set by Dietitians Australia. This credential matters because it tells you the advice comes from someone trained in evidence-based nutrition, not general wellness coaching.</p>
<p>Next, look for fertility experience beyond general nutrition. Fertility nutrition covers preconception, IVF preparation, PMOS (previously called PCOS) and endometriosis, and each of these areas has its own nuances. Someone who works in this space every week will ask different questions and tailor advice differently from someone who mostly sees general clients.</p>
<p>You also want someone who works alongside your fertility clinic and GP rather than in place of them. Nutrition supports medical care; it does not replace it. A good fertility dietitian will ask about your clinic&#39;s recommendations, fit them around your eating patterns and keep your care team in the loop when needed.</p>
<p>Practical factors matter too. Think about whether you want in-person consults in Brisbane or telehealth, how long the first consult is, and whether you will receive a written plan and an individualised supplement plan. These details shape how useful the experience feels in real life.</p>
<h2 id="what-does-a-fertility-dietitian-actually-help-with">What does a fertility dietitian actually help with?</h2>
<p>A fertility dietitian helps with preconception nutrition, IVF preparation, PMOS, endometriosis and male partner nutrition. The work is practical: reviewing what you currently eat, identifying gaps and building a pattern of eating that supports your body in the months before and during a cycle.</p>
<p>A dietitian does not diagnose or treat fertility conditions. Instead, she works alongside your clinic to make sure your food and supplement choices line up with your medical plan.</p>
<p>During a consult, a fertility dietitian typically reviews your overall eating pattern and key nutrients including folate, iodine, iron, omega-3 fats, vitamin D and zinc. She will also look at caffeine and alcohol, and at fish choices, since some large predatory fish such as shark, swordfish and marlin are higher in mercury.</p>
<p>An individualised approach matters because there is no single fertility diet that suits everyone. Your needs depend on your medical history, your cycle, your clinic&#39;s protocol and your lifestyle. A generic list of fertility foods is not the same as a plan built for you.</p>
<h2 id="what-is-the-difference-between-a-womens-health-dietitian-and-a-fertility-dietitian">What is the difference between a women&#39;s health dietitian and a fertility dietitian?</h2>
<p>The difference is one of scope. A fertility dietitian focuses on the preconception window and supporting you while you try to conceive, while a women&#39;s health dietitian covers the wider spectrum including pregnancy, hormonal health, PMOS, endometriosis and postpartum.</p>
<p>In practice, many dietitians work across both areas and the titles are not rigid. The most useful step is to ask about scope at your first enquiry, so you know whether the dietitian&#39;s experience matches your current stage.</p>
<h2 id="do-i-need-a-referral-to-see-a-fertility-dietitian-and-can-i-get-a-medicare-rebate">Do I need a referral to see a fertility dietitian, and can I get a Medicare rebate?</h2>
<p>No, you do not need a referral to book a private dietitian appointment. You can book directly.</p>
<p>A Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan. Eligibility depends on your GP&#39;s assessment and your health circumstances, so it is worth asking.</p>
<p>To ask your GP, book an appointment and explain that you would like to discuss whether you are eligible for a Chronic Condition Management Plan that includes a dietitian. Your GP will assess your situation and, if appropriate, prepare the plan.</p>

<h2 id="how-long-before-trying-to-conceive-should-i-see-a-fertility-dietitian">How long before trying to conceive should I see a fertility dietitian?</h2>
<p>The months before a cycle are the most useful window, and it is the one people most often skip. It takes around three months for eggs to mature, so this is when changes have time to become habits.</p>
<p>If you are planning to conceive, seeing a fertility dietitian around three to six months before you start trying gives you room to adjust your eating pattern, review supplements and build a routine that feels sustainable.</p>
<p>If you are preparing for IVF, the timing fits around your clinic&#39;s schedule. Many people see a dietitian before starting a cycle, and some continue through the cycle itself. What matters is starting early enough that the changes are in place before the busiest weeks arrive.</p>
<h2 id="what-are-the-key-nutrients-for-fertility-and-what-should-i-limit">What are the key nutrients for fertility, and what should I limit?</h2>
<p>Key nutrients before conception include folate, iodine, iron, omega-3 fats, vitamin D and zinc. Folic acid taken before and in early pregnancy reduces the risk of neural tube defects, and Australian guidance recommends iodine before and during pregnancy too. Iron and vitamin D are worth checking with a blood test rather than guessing.</p>
<p>When trying to conceive, it is sensible to limit alcohol, keep caffeine to around 200 mg a day or less, and choose lower-mercury fish, limiting shark, swordfish and marlin. Highly processed foods high in saturated fat, salt and added sugar are worth keeping to once in a blue moon rather than every day.</p>
<p>Food patterns matter more than any single food. The research on diet and fertility points at overall eating patterns rather than one magic ingredient. A varied, Mediterranean-style pattern built around vegetables, fish, legumes, wholegrains and healthy fats is a more useful target than chasing a superfood.</p>
<p>Supplement doses should be individualised, not self-prescribed from a forum. We use supplements to supplement your diet, not replace it, and some nutrients can be harmful in excess.</p>
<h2 id="can-a-fertility-dietitian-help-both-partners">Can a fertility dietitian help both partners?</h2>
<p>Yes. Male partner nutrition matters in the preconception window, and sperm health is influenced by overall eating pattern, nutrient status and lifestyle.</p>
<p>Partners are welcome to come along together, in person or by telehealth, so you both hear the same information at the same time and can prepare together.</p>
