There is a question coming up in my consults now that barely existed two years ago. A woman tells me she has been taking a GLP-1 medication, she is starting to think about trying for a baby, and someone has told her she will need to come off it. She usually knows roughly when. What nobody has talked to her about is what happens to her eating afterwards.

At the start of this month the first international consensus guidance on these medications across the reproductive years was published, so this feels like the right moment to write about the part that sits squarely in a dietitian’s lane.

## What the new guidance actually covers

The review appeared in *Obesity Reviews* in early August 2026, put together by researchers across Europe, North America and the Middle East. They worked through 34 studies and set out practical recommendations for contraception, preconception care, pregnancy, breastfeeding and the postpartum months.

Two things stood out to me. The first is refreshingly honest: the authors say close to half of the clinically important questions in this area still have no answer. The second is that the current evidence does not support using these medications during pregnancy or breastfeeding, which is exactly why the conversation about timing exists at all.

Before I go further, I want to be clear about something. Whether you take one of these medications, and when you stop, is a decision between you and the doctor who prescribes it. That is not my call to make and I would not try to make it. My work starts on the other side of it.

## The part nobody prepares you for

When the medication stops, appetite comes back. Sometimes gradually, often faster than expected, and almost always with a lot of feeling attached to it.

Women describe it to me as though something has gone wrong. They had months where eating felt quiet and manageable, and now hunger is loud again, and they read that as a personal failure. Nothing has gone wrong. Appetite returning is your physiology behaving exactly as you would expect it to.

The difficulty is that this usually lands in the same months you are being told to build your nutrition up for a pregnancy. So you have appetite returning, a lot of noise in your head about it, and a real nutritional job to do, all at the same time, and usually with nobody helping you tell one from the other. ## Why these particular months matter

The months before conception are worth protecting, because that is the window in which your body builds the stores it will draw on later. Folate, iodine and iron all sit near the front of that list, and the Australian [nutrient reference values](https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/nutrient-reference-values) lift the recommended intakes for each of them in pregnancy. If your overall intake has been low for a while, this is the window where that is worth addressing, rather than after the fact.

I want to hedge that properly. Nobody can promise you an outcome from an eating pattern, and I would be suspicious of anyone who did. What I can say is that going into a pregnancy with reasonable stores is a more comfortable place to start from than going in depleted.

## What we work on in that window

In practice, what I do with women coming off one of these medications is not a list of rules. It is usually four fairly ordinary things:

* Rebuilding a regular eating pattern, so that hunger is not arriving on an empty tank at four in the afternoon.
* Anchoring each meal around some protein, which tends to make appetite feel steadier through the day.
* Making sure carbohydrate is genuinely back in the picture, because it is usually the thing that quietly shrank while you were not hungry.
* Looking at the nutrients that matter most going into a pregnancy, and checking those with your GP rather than guessing at them.

None of it is dramatic. It is unglamorous, and it is the part that actually holds up.
