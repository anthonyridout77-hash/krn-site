## What does a fertility dietitian actually do?

A fertility dietitian gives you individual nutrition care for the months before conception, grounded in university training rather than whatever is trending online. An Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) has a recognised dietetics qualification and keeps up ongoing professional development, so the advice is evidence-based and specific to your health history.

A first appointment usually covers what you’re eating now, any supplements, your lifestyle, recent blood results, your cycle, and conditions such as PMOS (previously called PCOS) or endometriosis. It’s a much deeper look than a general healthy eating chat.

Fertility nutrition is for partners too. Alcohol, smoking, sleep, stress, activity and overall eating pattern can all play a part in sperm health, so I often bring partners into the conversation. It takes two, after all.

## How to choose a fertility dietitian in Sydney

Look first for the APD credential. You can check it through [Dietitians Australia](https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/), the professional body for dietitians in Australia. It’s a quick check, and worth doing before you book with anyone.

Next, look for an evidence-based approach. A good fit will explain the why behind their advice and talk about overall eating patterns rather than single hero foods or expensive protocols. If something comes with a big promise about outcomes, that promise is usually running ahead of the evidence.

Then think about the conditions that matter to you. If you have PMOS, endometriosis, a thyroid condition, or you’re heading into IVF or egg freezing, ask whether the dietitian works with these regularly.

Finally, the practical things count. How long is the first consult? Do you get a written plan? Can your partner come? Will they write back to your GP or fertility specialist? These small details make a big difference to how supported you feel.

## Can telehealth work for fertility nutrition?

Yes. Video consults work really well for this kind of care. We can go through your blood results and supplements, look at your usual week of eating, and set up follow-up reviews, all from your couch. You can share documents and talk through your cycle history just as you would in a clinic room.

For women in Sydney, that means you can choose a dietitian on fit and experience rather than postcode. No traffic, no parking, and no rushing out of work.

## What eating pattern has the strongest evidence when trying to conceive?

The research points to overall eating patterns rather than any one food. Mediterranean-style eating, rich in vegetables, fruit, legumes, wholegrains, fish, olive oil and nuts, comes up again and again in reviews of diet and fertility ([Gaskins and Chavarro, Am J Obstet Gynecol](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajog.2017.10.003)). No single food in that list is doing the work on its own. It’s the whole pattern.

Before conception, the priorities are folate and iodine (Australian advice is to start a supplement with both before you fall pregnant), along with vitamin D, iron and B12 if your levels are low, plus food safety habits. [Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/planning-for-pregnancy) has a good overview of planning for pregnancy.

It’s also worth being honest about what nutrition can’t do. Good nutrition supports egg quality and your overall health going into pregnancy, but it can’t increase the number of eggs you have. Setting realistic expectations is part of good care.

## Can nutrition help with PMOS, endometriosis or thyroid issues?

Nutrition can play a supportive role, though the evidence differs for each. In PMOS (previously called PCOS), insulin resistance sits behind many of the symptoms, including irregular cycles. [Jean Hailes](https://www.jeanhailes.org.au/health-a-z/pcos) notes that healthy eating and regular activity are first-line management, and there is no single special diet that suits everyone.

With endometriosis, a low FODMAP diet may help with the gut symptoms that often come along with it, though it doesn’t treat the endometriosis itself. It’s a structured three-phase process that’s done with a dietitian, and [Monash University FODMAP](https://www.monashfodmap.com/) has the background.

For thyroid conditions, I work closely with your GP or endocrinologist, especially around iodine and the timing of medication with food.

## Do I need supplements, or a referral?

Supplement needs are individual. I look at your intake, bloods and stage of the journey before suggesting anything, and I err on the side of caution: start with what you need, check for interactions, and review it in 6 to 12 weeks.

You don’t need a referral to see a dietitian. If you have a chronic condition such as PMOS, a Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan, so it’s worth asking your GP.
