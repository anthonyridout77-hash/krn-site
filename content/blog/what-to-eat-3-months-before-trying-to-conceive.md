If you’re starting to think about a baby, the next three months are a really good place to put a little bit of energy into food. It takes about three months to build your eggs, and a full sperm cycle takes roughly 64 days, so what you both eat now is what you’re building with. I guess the key thing is this isn’t about a perfect diet. It’s a handful of simple little switches that give you the most nutrition bang for your buck, and I’ll walk you through them here.

*By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed October 2026.*

## Why does the 3 months before conception matter so much?

The preconception period is usually described as the months, up to a year, before you fall pregnant. Three months is the practical window most people work with, because it lines up with the biology of both eggs and sperm. Eggs mature over roughly three months before ovulation, and sperm take a little over two months to develop, so your partner’s food matters in this window too.

A major review in The Lancet by Stephenson and colleagues found that nutrition and lifestyle before pregnancy can influence the health of both mum and baby, partly through weight and partly through specific nutrients. What we do know is that the months before conception are a real window to support future health, not a time to wait for a positive test.

If you have an underlying health condition, irregular cycles or PMOS (previously called PCOS), it can be worth starting a little earlier and bringing your GP in from the start.

## What does a preconception diet plan actually look like day to day?

It looks a lot like a good baseline diet, done a bit more intentionally. Think Mediterranean style: plenty of vegetables and fruit, wholegrains, legumes, nuts, olive oil and fish two to three times a week.

Aim for half a plate of veg at lunch and dinner and a daily handful of berries. Choose wholegrain where you can; intact rolled or steel cut oats rather than quick oats, grainy bread rather than white. Your body has to munch and crunch those down, which gives you more staying power.

Dairy or a calcium-fortified alternative most days helps with calcium and iodine. At snack time, pair a protein with a carb and some fibre, like yoghurt and berries or wholegrain crackers with cheese.

Keep it practical and flexible. A preconception diet is a pattern, not a protocol, and it should fit around your life rather than take it over.

## Which foods are worth putting on your plate each week?

Leafy greens like spinach give you folate and some iron. Lentils, chickpeas and beans are a powerhouse, with folate, protein, iron and fibre in one tin.

Berries bring antioxidants and vitamin C, which also helps you absorb iron from plant foods. Oily fish like salmon and sardines bring omega-3 fats, and walnuts (they even look like a little brain) are a handy plant source.

Other useful nutrition sprinkles include Brazil nuts for selenium, pumpkin and sunflower seeds, avocado, olives, extra virgin olive oil and ground flaxseed. Keep a mixed nut bag in the pantry and it becomes a no-brainer sort of snack. None of these is a wonder food in itself; it’s the overall pattern that counts.

## How much protein and healthy fat should you be having?

Aim to include some protein at every meal and most snacks. A mix of plant proteins (beans, lentils, tofu, nuts and seeds) and animal proteins (eggs, fish, lean meat, dairy) works well for most women.

As a rough guide, one serve of protein is two eggs, a cup of cooked lentils or beans, a small tin of fish, about a palm of cooked meat, or a small handful of nuts.

For healthy fats, think a drizzle of olive oil on your veg, a quarter of an avocado, a sprinkle of seeds and fish a few times a week.

These are starting points to adapt, not rigid rules. Your needs depend on your body, your history and your lifestyle.

## Do you need supplements before trying to conceive?

Yes, two of them for almost everyone. Folic acid helps prevent neural tube defects such as spina bifida. Australian advice is 400 micrograms (0.4 mg) of folic acid a day, starting at least one month before you try and continuing through the first three months of pregnancy. Many women start it as soon as they stop contraception. Some women, for example with diabetes or a higher BMI, may be advised to take a higher dose, so check with your GP.

Iodine is the other one. The NHMRC recommends a 150 microgram iodine supplement a day for women who are considering pregnancy, pregnant or breastfeeding. If you have a thyroid condition, talk to your doctor first.

Iron and vitamin D are worth checking with a blood test rather than guessing. I err on the side of caution with supplements, because sometimes there is too much of a good thing, and stacking several products can double up. We use supplements to supplement your diet, not replace it. A dietitian can tailor this to your bloods, your history and what you’re already taking.

## Does your weight affect your chances of conceiving?

Weight can influence fertility in both directions. Being underweight or above a healthy weight can affect hormone production, the menstrual cycle and sperm health.

Nutrient gaps before conception, in women and men, are also linked with how a baby grows, including placenta development and birth weight. That’s why I focus on what’s on the plate first.

I never change a diet to focus on weight loss by itself. If weight is something you want to work on, we come at it from a multi angle, in a really safe way, with changes that are achievable, that stay for good and that carry into pregnancy. Weight is only one indicator of health; energy levels and bloods matter too.

## What should you limit or avoid while trying to conceive?

Caffeine is worth watching. One regular coffee is about 100 mg, and most pregnancy guidance sits at about 200 mg a day. A large flat white can be around three shots, so add a bought iced coffee and you can be at four to five shots without noticing. Counting your shots now makes pregnancy easier.

Alcohol is the clear one. Australia’s alcohol guidelines say women who are pregnant or planning a pregnancy should not drink alcohol. It’s worth your partner cutting back too, given the sperm cycle.

Irregular periods can have several causes, including PMOS, an underactive thyroid, and stress. Nutrition sits alongside medical care, not instead of it, so irregular cycles deserve a proper chat with your GP.

## Key Takeaways

* Three months is the practical preconception window, because eggs mature over about three months and a sperm cycle takes roughly 64 days.
* A Mediterranean style pattern works well: half a plate of veg, berries, wholegrains, legumes, nuts, olive oil and fish two to three times a week.
* Take 400 micrograms of folic acid a day from at least one month before trying, plus a 150 microgram iodine supplement, unless your doctor advises otherwise.
* Check iron and vitamin D with a blood test rather than guessing, and avoid stacking supplements.
* Australian guidelines say women planning a pregnancy should not drink alcohol; keep caffeine to about 200 mg a day and count the shots in bought coffees.
* Weight can affect hormones, cycles and sperm health; focus on food quality and changes that last.
