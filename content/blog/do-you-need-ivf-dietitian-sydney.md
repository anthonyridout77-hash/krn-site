If you are preparing for IVF in Sydney, you have probably been handed a lot of advice about food already. Some from your clinic, some from a forum late at night. You don’t need a dietitian in Sydney to get good support with it. I work with women and couples all over Australia by telehealth from Brisbane. Nutrition supports your fertility clinic’s care and never replaces it.

*By Katrina Ridout, Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), Brisbane. Reviewed October 2026.*

## What does an IVF dietitian actually do?

An IVF dietitian looks at the whole picture: your overall eating pattern, key nutrients, supplements, alcohol, caffeine, sleep and activity. Not a single fertility food, and not a rigid protocol handed to everyone. The work is practical and fits around your treatment timeline.

Food first, then supplements only where you need them. I err on the side of caution with supplements, check them against your clinic’s plan, and review them in 6 to 12 weeks.

## How do you prepare for IVF with nutrition?

The months before a cycle are where most of the useful work happens. The research on diet and fertility is largely observational, and it points at overall eating patterns, such as Mediterranean-style eating, rather than any one hero food ([Gaskins and Chavarro](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajog.2017.10.003)). Consistency matters far more than perfection.

In practice I look at regular meals with enough protein and fibre, iron and folate-rich foods, omega-3s from fish two to three times a week, caffeine (one coffee is about 100 mg, and around 200 mg a day is a sensible cap), alcohol, and sleep and stress.

During the stimulation weeks, keep it simple. Appetite can swing all over the place and bloating is common, so this is not the moment for new food rules. Regular meals, protein across the day, fluids, and a few freezer meals for the nights you’ve been at the clinic since seven.

## Does your partner’s nutrition matter?

Yes. Sperm health is influenced by overall eating pattern, alcohol, smoking, sleep and activity, and sperm take around three months to develop. The [NHMRC](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/health-advice/alcohol) advises that not drinking is safest when planning a pregnancy. Couples often find changes easier when they make them together.

## What if you are also navigating PMOS or endometriosis?

PMOS (previously called PCOS) and endometriosis are common reasons women come to me before IVF. With PMOS, we often focus on steadier blood sugar by pairing carbs with protein and fibre. With endometriosis, a structured low FODMAP approach may help gut symptoms, and [Monash University](https://www.monashfodmap.com/) explains how it works. It doesn’t treat the endometriosis itself, and it works well with a dietitian guiding you.

These plans are individual, so what worked for someone else may not suit your history, your bloods or your clinic’s protocol.

## Medicare rebates and referrals

You don’t need a referral to book with a dietitian. If you have a chronic condition, your GP may set up a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan, and a Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on that plan. Private health extras may also contribute, so check with your fund.

## How to choose an IVF dietitian in Sydney

Look first for the Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD) credential. You can check it through [Dietitians Australia](https://dietitiansaustralia.org.au/). It only takes a minute, and it tells you the person has a recognised dietetics degree and keeps up ongoing professional development.

Next, look for an evidence-based approach. A good fit will explain the why behind their advice and talk about overall eating patterns rather than hero foods, detoxes or expensive protocols. If something comes with a big promise about outcomes, that promise is usually running ahead of the evidence.

Then think about what matters for you. If you have PMOS, endometriosis, a thyroid condition or a history of restrictive eating, look for someone with experience in those areas who will work alongside your clinic. Ask how often they see this, and whether they will talk to your doctors.

Finally, the practical things count. How long is the first consult? Do you get a written plan? Can your partner come along? Is telehealth an option for reviews? These small details make a big difference to how supported you feel.
