<p>If you&#8217;re over 35 and trying for a baby, I want to start with something reassuring. Fertility does change with age, slowly after 30 and more noticeably after 35, and no food can turn back that clock. But what you eat is still one of the things within your control, and I guess the key thing is that small, steady changes made now give your body a good baseline to work from. It takes around three months to build your eggs, so the next few months really count.</p>

<h2 id="what-actually-changes-about-fertility-after-35">What actually changes about fertility after 35?</h2>
<p>Fertility declines gradually after 30, steepens somewhat after 35 and becomes sharper again in the forties, according to <a href="https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/fertility-and-conception/fertility/trying-for-pregnancy-after-35">Pregnancy, Birth and Baby</a>. This is a gradual shift, not a cliff edge, but it is a real biological pattern.</p>
<p>Both the number and the quality of eggs change with age, which affects the likelihood of a healthy pregnancy. Pregnancy complications that become more common with age include pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, miscarriage and chromosomal conditions such as Down syndrome, according to <a href="https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/fertility-and-conception/fertility/trying-for-pregnancy-after-35">Pregnancy, Birth and Baby</a>. This is why preconception care matters so much.</p>
<p>Miscarriage risk also rises with age. A large Norwegian register study found the risk was lowest in the late twenties and climbed steadily from the mid thirties onwards (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l869">Magnus and colleagues, BMJ 2019</a>). Even so, most pregnancies in women in their late thirties and early forties continue.</p>
<p>If you are 35 or older and have been trying to get pregnant for 6 months or more, see your GP. That is the point where a conversation about fertility testing and next steps is useful.</p>
<h2 id="which-eating-pattern-has-the-strongest-evidence-for-fertility">Which eating pattern has the strongest evidence for fertility?</h2>
<p>A Mediterranean-style eating pattern has the most positive evidence for female fertility. It is rich in dietary fibre, omega-3 fatty acids, plant-based protein, vitamins and minerals, according to a <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8634384/">2021 review of female fertility and nutrition</a>. In real life that means plenty of vegetables, fruit, legumes, wholegrains, nuts, seeds, olive oil and fish, with smaller amounts of red meat and discretionary foods.</p>
<p>Diets high in trans fats, refined carbohydrates and added sugars are associated with poorer fertility outcomes, according to the <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8634384/">same review</a>. The evidence points to overall eating patterns rather than any single hero food, so the goal is a way of eating you could keep up for the long haul rather than a short-term fix.</p>
<p>Your partner matters too. Sperm take around three months to develop as well, so eating well together is a practical way to support both sides of the equation.</p>
<p>This research is largely observational. It shows associations rather than cause and effect, but the consistency of the findings makes a Mediterranean-style pattern a sensible foundation while you are trying to conceive.</p>
<h2 id="which-nutrients-and-supplements-matter-most-when-preparing-for-pregnancy">Which nutrients and supplements matter most when preparing for pregnancy?</h2>
<p>Folic acid has the clearest preconception guidance. Folic acid, iodine and vitamin D supplements are recommended around pregnancy, according to <a href="https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/vitamins-and-supplements-during-pregnancy">Pregnancy, Birth and Baby</a>. The usual advice is a folic acid supplement of at least 400 micrograms a day, started at least a month before you conceive and continued through the first three months of pregnancy. Timing matters because the neural tube forms very early, often before you know you are pregnant.</p>
<p>Iodine is the other one worth knowing about. A supplement of 150 micrograms a day is generally recommended for women planning a pregnancy, but if you have a thyroid condition, talk to your GP before starting one.</p>
<p>Omega-3 fats are easiest to get from food first. Oily fish such as salmon, sardines and mackerel are useful sources, alongside plant sources like walnuts, flaxseeds and chia seeds, according to the <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8634384/">2021 review</a>.</p>
<p>More is not better when it comes to supplements. We use supplements to supplement your diet, not replace it. Your needs depend on your blood results, medical history, diet and fertility treatment plan, so an individualised plan is far more useful than a generic supplement stack.</p>
<h2 id="how-much-caffeine-and-alcohol-is-okay-when-trying-to-conceive">How much caffeine and alcohol is okay when trying to conceive?</h2>
<p>Caffeine adds up quicker than most people expect. One shot of espresso has roughly 60 to 100 mg, and tea, cola and energy drinks all count. I suggest keeping your total under about 200 mg a day while you are trying, which is the same cap used in pregnancy, so there is nothing to change when you get a positive test. Count your shots first, then decide whether to shrink, taper or swap. Completely up to you.</p>
<p>Alcohol is simpler. The <a href="https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/health-advice/alcohol">NHMRC guidelines</a> say that women who are pregnant or planning a pregnancy should not drink alcohol, to reduce the risk of harm to their unborn child.</p>
<p>The practical approach is to notice your current intake and make small reductions where they feel manageable. You do not need to cut everything out overnight, and a rigid all-or-nothing approach is rarely sustainable.</p>
<h2 id="does-how-much-you-move-and-weigh-affect-fertility">Does how much you move and weigh affect fertility?</h2>
<p>Yes, movement and body weight both appear to influence fertility. Regular movement supports blood flow, blood sugar and mood, and it sits alongside good nutrition as part of the broader picture, not as a standalone fertility treatment.</p>
<p>Movement does not need to be intense or time-consuming. Walking, swimming, yoga or a short strength session all count. Frequency beats heroics: two or three sessions a week will feel easier and do more than one big effort.</p>
<p>If you are unsure where to start, a gentle increase in daily steps is a reasonable first step. A lap of the shopping centre counts. It&#8217;s all valid.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-you-time-sex-and-track-ovulation-when-trying-over-35">How do you time sex and track ovulation when trying over 35?</h2>
<p>The chance of getting pregnant is highest with sex 1 to 2 days before ovulation, according to <a href="https://www.pregnancybirthbaby.org.au/fertility-and-conception/fertility/trying-for-pregnancy-after-35">Pregnancy, Birth and Baby</a>. Sperm can survive for several days, which is why the days before ovulation matter more than the day itself.</p>
<p>Ovulation predictor kits detect the surge in luteinising hormone that usually comes a day or so before ovulation. They can help you find your fertile window, but they are a tool rather than a promise.</p>
<p>Cycle tracking is useful, but it is not a substitute for medical advice. If you have been trying for 6 months or more and you are 35 or older, your GP or fertility specialist can offer more detailed monitoring and guidance on timing.</p>
