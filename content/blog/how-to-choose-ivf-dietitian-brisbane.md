## What does an IVF dietitian actually do?

An IVF dietitian looks at your overall eating pattern, your nutrient status and the safety of your supplements, alongside your fertility clinic’s care. Nutrition supports medical treatment and never replaces it. The work usually covers preconception nutrition, egg and sperm health, cycle regularity, and support through stimulation, transfer and the two-week wait.

Any APD can help with healthy eating, but one with extra fertility and pregnancy training understands the timing and demands of an IVF cycle. That matters because IVF involves medications, procedures and waiting periods where food choices and supplements need to be considered in context.

The practical work often includes reviewing what you eat across a typical week, finding the nutrition gaps, checking supplement forms and doses, and making changes that fit your life. It isn’t about a rigid protocol.

## What should you look for in an IVF dietitian in Brisbane?

Credentials come first. Look for an Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD), the credential run by Dietitians Australia, plus fertility-specific professional development. The APD credential means the dietitian has met national standards for university training and keeps up ongoing education.

Experience with your situation matters too. If you have PMOS (previously called PCOS), endometriosis, unexplained infertility, male factor concerns or a history of miscarriage, ask whether the dietitian regularly works with that. Support should be tailored to you, not a one-size-fits-all plan.

A non-diet, weight-inclusive approach is worth looking for, especially if you feel wary of weight-focused advice. You also want honest, evidence-based language rather than promises about outcomes. Nobody can promise a food will make a cycle work. Practical fit counts as well: whether you’d prefer in-person consults in Brisbane or telehealth from home.

## What should you ask before you book an appointment?

Ask whether the dietitian is an APD and what fertility-specific training they’ve done. The credential tells you about their standards, and the training tells you whether they understand IVF timing, medications and supplement safety.

Ask how they work alongside your fertility specialist and clinic, and whether they’ll write back to your care team. Coordination matters because supplement changes and dietary shifts can interact with treatment. It’s also fair to ask what a first appointment involves, how long it runs and what you’ll receive afterwards, such as a written plan or supplement schedule.

Ask whether telehealth is available if you’re outside Brisbane or juggling a busy week, and how Medicare eligibility under a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan works. Clarify these before booking so there are no surprises.

## How does nutrition for IVF differ from nutrition for natural conception?

The foundations are much the same. Preconception nutrition for natural conception and IVF both focus on overall eating patterns, folate, iodine, iron, omega-3 fats and a Mediterranean-style way of eating.

IVF adds timing and treatment. You’re preparing in the months before a cycle, supporting your body through stimulation and transfer, and managing symptoms along the way. It takes about three months to build your eggs, so the most useful work usually happens before a cycle starts, which is the window people most often skip past.

Research on diet and fertility is largely observational and points to overall eating patterns rather than single hero foods. So the goal is a sustainable way of eating that supports your nutrient status and wellbeing across the cycle, not a short-term fix.

## Can a dietitian help with PMOS, endometriosis or male fertility before IVF?

Yes. PMOS (previously called PCOS) and endometriosis each affect fertility differently, so nutrition support is tailored to the condition and to how it interacts with your treatment.

Male fertility matters too. Sperm health is a shared part of the picture, which is why some couples come in together. A dietitian can review nutrient intake, lifestyle factors and supplement safety for both partners.

Conditions such as thyroid disorders, coeliac disease and inflammation can sit alongside fertility care and are worth raising with your team. A fertility-trained dietitian can help make sure these are considered alongside your IVF cycle and work with your GP or specialist where needed.

## Does Medicare cover seeing an IVF dietitian?

A Medicare rebate may be available for eligible patients on a GP Chronic Condition Management Plan (GPCCMP), which your GP sets up and refers through. It isn’t automatic and depends on your clinical circumstances.

The number of subsidised allied health visits each year is limited, so it’s worth planning how you use them, perhaps some before a cycle and some during it. You can’t claim both Medicare and private health insurance for the same session.

If you think you may be eligible, start with your GP. They can assess whether a GPCCMP is right for you and provide the referral.
