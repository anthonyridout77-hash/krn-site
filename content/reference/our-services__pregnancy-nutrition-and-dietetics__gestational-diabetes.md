## If you are diagnosed with gestational diabetes during pregnancy, don’t panic, we’re here to help.

It is not uncommon for women to develop Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) during pregnancy. In fact, around 1 in 6 Australian women are diagnosed, so if this is part of your journey, you are definitely not alone.

### Why do women get gestational diabetes?
Gestational diabetes usually goes away after giving birth, but if it is not well managed with diet, exercise and sometimes medication, it can cause complications for both you and your baby. It can also increase your risk of developing type 2 diabetes later in life.

### How does gestational diabetes affect my baby?
With GDM, babies are more likely to be larger than average (large for gestational age), which can sometimes lead to complications such as an emergency caesarean section or pre-term labour. If blood sugars are not well controlled, GDM can also increase the risk of your baby experiencing blood sugar imbalances, jaundice, breathing difficulties, or long-term metabolic issues. The good news is that with the right care and nutrition, these risks can be significantly reduced.

### How is gestational diabetes diagnosed?
All women are screened for gestational diabetes between 24 and 28 weeks of pregnancy. This is usually done with blood tests and an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), where you drink a sweet liquid and your blood sugar is measured over time. Your doctor, midwife or dietitian can guide you through the process and explain your results.

### Quick Tips for Managing GDM
Here are some simple steps that can make a big difference:

- Eat regular meals and snacks to keep blood sugar levels stable
- Choose wholegrain and low-GI carbohydrates for slow, steady energy
- Pair carbs with protein and healthy fats to balance blood sugar
- Stay active with gentle exercise like walking, if recommended by your doctor
- Monitor your blood sugar levels as advised by your healthcare team
- Stay hydrated with plenty of water throughout the day

## Frequently Asked Questions

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is relatively common, affecting around 1 in 6 pregnancies in Australia. The likelihood of developing it can vary depending on factors such as age, ethnicity and existing health conditions. Because of this, all women are recommended to be screened for GDM between 24 and 28 weeks of pregnancy. Early detection means it can be managed well, helping to keep both you and your baby healthy.

Gestational diabetes (GDM) can increase the risk of complications for both mother and baby if it is not well managed. For mums, this may include a higher chance of preeclampsia, the need for a caesarean section, and a greater risk of developing type 2 diabetes or cardiovascular disease later in life. For babies, possible complications include being larger than average (macrosomia), low blood sugar after birth, breathing difficulties, jaundice, and an increased risk of obesity or type 2 diabetes as they grow older.

The encouraging news is that with the right care, including healthy lifestyle changes, blood sugar monitoring, and medication if needed, these risks can be significantly reduced. Proper management of GDM helps protect your health and gives your baby the best possible start in life.

Gestational diabetes (GDM) can often be managed effectively through diet and lifestyle changes. A dietitian can help create a meal plan that regulates carbohydrate intake, ensuring stable blood sugar levels. This typically involves eating smaller, more frequent meals rich in fibre and nutrients and avoiding sugary foods and beverages. Regular physical activity, as recommended by a healthcare provider, can also help manage blood sugar levels. Monitoring blood sugar levels regularly and taking any prescribed medication as directed are important parts of managing GDM. In some cases, insulin therapy may be necessary to control blood sugar levels. Regular prenatal check-ups are essential to monitor the health of both the mother and the baby.

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a type of diabetes that develops specifically during pregnancy. It occurs when the body is unable to produce enough insulin to meet the increased needs brought on by pregnancy. Insulin is a hormone that helps regulate blood sugar (glucose) levels.

During pregnancy, the placenta produces hormones that can interfere with insulin’s action in the mother’s body. This can lead to elevated blood sugar levels, a condition known as hyperglycaemia. Gestational diabetes typically develops around the 24th to 28th week of pregnancy, when the body’s insulin resistance increases due to hormonal changes.

Gestational diabetes is different from type 1 diabetes (an autoimmune condition where the body doesn’t produce insulin) and type 2 diabetes (where the body doesn’t use insulin properly or doesn’t produce enough insulin). Gestational diabetes usually resolves after childbirth, but it increases the risk of developing type 2 diabetes later in life for both the mother and sometimes the child.

Managing gestational diabetes involves closely monitoring blood sugar levels, making dietary adjustments, engaging in regular physical activity, and sometimes using medications or insulin to keep blood sugar levels within a healthy range. This management is crucial to reducing the risk of complications for both the mother and the baby during pregnancy and childbirth. Regular prenatal care and monitoring by healthcare providers are essential to ensuring the best outcomes for both mother and baby.

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is managed through a comprehensive approach that includes dietary modifications, regular physical activity, blood sugar monitoring, and, if necessary, medication or insulin therapy under medical supervision. A balanced diet emphasising whole grains, fruits, vegetables, lean proteins, and healthy fats helps regulate blood sugar levels. Regular exercise, tailored to individual fitness levels, improves insulin sensitivity and aids in blood sugar control. Monitoring blood sugar levels throughout the day provides insights into how well lifestyle changes are working, guiding adjustments as needed.

Healthcare providers, including dietitians like Katrina Ridout, play a critical role in providing personalised nutrition counselling, creating effective meal plans, and offering ongoing support to manage GDM effectively. By collaborating with healthcare professionals, women with gestational diabetes can optimise their health outcomes and reduce risks for both themselves and their babies during pregnancy and beyond.
