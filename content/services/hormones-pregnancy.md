The hormonal changes that come with pregnancy can be surprising. They affect your mood, your digestion, how your body responds to activity and how you feel day to day. Food cannot switch pregnancy hormones on or off, but what and how you eat can make many of their side effects easier to live with. This page is part of Kat's [pregnancy nutrition](/our-services/pregnancy-nutrition-and-dietetics/) support.

## What pregnancy hormones do

Supportive daily habits around nutrition, movement, sleep and self-care help your body feel prepared for pregnancy. Once you are pregnant, hormones such as hCG, progesterone, oestrogen, relaxin and prolactin rise and fall to support the placenta, maintain the uterine lining, help your baby grow and prepare your body for labour, birth and breastfeeding. They also influence your metabolism, appetite, mood and immune system.

The two main hormones that increase during pregnancy are oestrogen and progesterone.

Oestrogen rises to help the uterus and placenta:

- Transfer nutrients
- Support your baby's development and maturing
- Improve the formation of blood vessels

Progesterone rises to:

- Aid the laxity of ligaments and joints throughout the body
- Help internal structures such as the uterus expand in size
- Help those structures accommodate and develop along with your baby

When hormones are out of balance it can be harder to get pregnant. In pregnancy, imbalance can show up as sudden changes in body temperature, indigestion and bloating, skin blemishes, mood swings and fluctuating stress, irritability and trouble concentrating.

## How nutrition supports you through the changes

Below are the changes Kat sees most often, and the practical strategies used for each.

### Nausea and food aversions (mostly weeks 6 to 14)

Rising hCG and oestrogen are linked with morning sickness, which can happen at any time of day. Strategies that often help:

- Small, frequent meals and snacks so your stomach is never completely empty
- Plain, dry or cold foods such as crackers, toast, rice cakes, fruit and yoghurt
- Ginger in tea, biscuits or lollies
- Sipping fluids between meals rather than with them
- Taking your prenatal supplement with food or at night if it makes you queasy

If you cannot keep fluids down, are losing weight or feel dizzy, see your GP or midwife promptly. This may be hyperemesis gravidarum and needs medical care.

### Heartburn, bloating and constipation

Progesterone relaxes smooth muscle throughout the body, which slows digestion and loosens the valve at the top of the stomach. Eating smaller meals, staying upright for an hour after eating, limiting very rich or spicy meals at night, and building up fibre slowly alongside plenty of water can all help.

### Blood glucose, hunger and cravings

From the second trimester, placental hormones make the body more resistant to insulin so more glucose reaches your baby. For some women this leads to gestational diabetes, which is screened for at 24 to 28 weeks. Pairing carbohydrate foods with protein, choosing wholegrains and spreading carbohydrate across the day help keep energy steadier. Read more in [Gestational diabetes: just diagnosed, what now?](/blogs/gestational-diabetes-just-diagnosed-what-now/)

### Thyroid hormones and iodine

Your thyroid works harder in pregnancy, and your baby relies on your thyroid hormone for brain development. Australian guidelines recommend a daily 150 microgram iodine supplement for women who are pregnant, breastfeeding or trying to conceive. If you have a thyroid condition, check with your doctor before starting iodine.

### Mood, sleep and energy

Tiredness and mood changes are common as hormones shift. Low iron, vitamin B12 or vitamin D can add to fatigue, so it is worth checking your blood results. Regular meals, enough protein, omega-3 rich foods such as oily fish (two to three serves a week of low-mercury fish) and good hydration all support steadier energy. See [Iron in pregnancy](/blogs/iron-in-pregnancy-tired-before-anaemia/).

## Which foods support hormonal balance?

A balanced diet with healthy fats (avocados, nuts, seeds, oily fish), high-fibre foods, quality protein and a variety of fruit and vegetables supports your body through its natural hormone shifts. Omega-3 fats, B vitamins and magnesium are particularly helpful, and regular meals that mix complex carbohydrates, protein and healthy fats help keep blood sugar steady.
