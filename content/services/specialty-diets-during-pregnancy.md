Eating a balanced diet matters for your baby's development, but it can feel harder if you follow a specialty diet. Whether you are vegan or vegetarian, eat low-carbohydrate, or have restrictions such as gluten-free, dairy-free or low-FODMAP, it helps to understand how those choices affect your nutritional balance. This page is part of Kat's [pregnancy nutrition](/our-services/pregnancy-nutrition-and-dietetics/) support.

## Do I need to eat more now that I am expecting?

The short answer is no. "Eating for two" is a myth. While your body needs more vitamins and minerals during pregnancy, your overall energy needs do not increase by much. You do not need any extra energy in the first trimester, and in the second and third trimesters only a modest amount more each day, roughly a banana and a small tub of yoghurt. What matters most is the quality of what you eat: nourishment, not quantity.

## Key nutrients in pregnancy

### Protein

Protein supports the growth of your baby's tissues, organs and brain, as well as your own breast and uterine tissue and the increase in blood supply. Most pregnant women need around 75 to 100 grams a day, about 2 to 3 palm-sized serves of protein-rich foods. Good sources include fully cooked fish or seafood, chicken, lean beef, lamb, pork, tofu, legumes and nuts.

### Folate

Folate is a B-group vitamin vital for healthy growth, especially the formation of your baby's nervous system, so it is critical in the months before pregnancy and the first trimester. It is found in green leafy vegetables such as spinach, broccoli and asparagus, fruits like strawberries, oranges and bananas, and legumes. In Australia, folic acid is added to all wheat flour used in bread-making, so bread is an easy way to add intake unless you bake your own or buy organic. Because most women do not get enough from food alone, a daily 500 microgram folic acid supplement is recommended. Some women need more, so check with your dietitian or doctor.

### Iron

Iron helps your body make red blood cells that carry oxygen to you and your baby, and your baby stores iron to last the first five to six months after birth. Too little iron in pregnancy raises the risk of pre-term delivery or low birth weight. The recommended intake is 27 mg a day, about 9 mg more than for non-pregnant women. Iron from animal foods is absorbed easily; plant sources such as beans, lentils and leafy greens are absorbed less well, but pairing them with vitamin C-rich foods like oranges, strawberries or tomatoes improves absorption considerably. This is especially important for vegetarian and vegan mums-to-be.

### Iodine

Iodine is key for your baby's brain development and for producing thyroid hormones. Too little can increase the risk of developmental delays and thyroid problems. Seafood, seaweed, eggs, meat, dairy and most commercially made breads (including most gluten-free breads, but not organic or some yeast-free breads) are good sources. Do not increase salt intake just for iodine. Women planning pregnancy, pregnant or breastfeeding are advised to take a daily pregnancy multivitamin containing 150 micrograms of iodine.

### Vitamin D

Vitamin D helps your baby's bones and teeth form and supports your own muscle strength. The main source is sunlight, with smaller amounts in oily fish, egg yolks, margarine and some milks. You may be at higher risk of deficiency if you have darker skin, cover most of your body with clothing, or spend most of your time indoors. If so, ask your GP for a blood test; they can recommend a supplement for pregnancy and breastfeeding.

### Calcium

Calcium supports your baby's bones and teeth, particularly in the third trimester. Your body absorbs calcium more efficiently in pregnancy, so your daily requirement does not increase: 1,000 mg a day for women aged 19 to 50, and 1,300 mg for adolescents. Milk, cheese, yoghurt and calcium-fortified soy milk are reliable sources.

### Vitamin B6

Your blood volume increases in pregnancy, so you need more vitamin B6. Low levels have been linked with pre-eclampsia, high blood pressure and severe nausea and vomiting, and B6 may play a role in your baby's brain development. It is easy to get from fortified breakfast cereals, vegetables, fruit and meat.

## Vegan and vegetarian pregnancies

A well-planned vegan or vegetarian diet can provide all the nutrients needed for a healthy pregnancy. The nutrients to watch are protein, vitamin B12, iron, omega-3 fats, calcium and overall energy. Vitamin B12 is found mainly in animal foods, so vegans, and vegetarians who do not regularly eat dairy, eggs or fortified foods, should consider a B12 supplement. Fortified plant milks, breakfast cereals and nutritional yeast help, but their B12 content varies. Vegans still need to avoid certain fish, raw or undercooked eggs, unpasteurised juice and some herbal teas. A poorly planned plant-based diet may fall short, so planning with a dietitian matters.
