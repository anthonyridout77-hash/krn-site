What you eat in the months before and during pregnancy has a lasting impact on your baby’s growth and development, as well as your own health. If you have specific dietary needs, balancing your nutrition becomes even more important to support a healthy pregnancy and give your baby the best start in life.
Eating a healthy, balanced diet is one of the best ways to optimise your baby’s development, but this can feel more challenging if you follow a speciality diet. Pregnancy is such an exciting time, but it can also feel overwhelming. Many mums worry about making the “right” choices and feel the weight of responsibility for their growing baby. That’s completely natural — and you don’t have to figure it out alone.
Whether you follow a vegan or vegetarian lifestyle, eat low-carbohydrate, or have dietary restrictions such as gluten-free, dairy-free or low-FODMAP, it’s important to understand how these choices affect your nutritional balance. With the right support, you can feel confident that you and your baby are getting everything you need for a healthy pregnancy.

### Do I need to eat more now that I'm expecting?
The short answer is no. The old saying “eating for two” is a myth. While your body does need more vitamins and minerals during pregnancy, your overall energy needs don’t increase by much.
What matters most is the quality of what you eat. By focusing on nutrient-rich foods, you can make sure you and your baby get everything you need for healthy growth and development, without necessarily eating larger portions.
It’s about nourishment, not quantity. I can help you make it simple and practical.

### How much energy do pregnant women need each day?
Every woman’s energy needs in pregnancy are different. They depend on things like your activity level, stage of pregnancy, body composition and pre-pregnancy BMI.
As a general guide, if you were at a healthy BMI before becoming pregnant, you’ll need somewhere between 9,200 kJ (2,200 calories) and 12,130 kJ (2,900 calories) each day while expecting.
You don’t need any extra calories in the first trimester. In the second and third trimesters, you only need around an extra 1,250 kJ (300 calories) per day — roughly the same as adding a banana and a small tub of yoghurt.

### What key nutrients do I need?
When you are growing a baby, your body is working harder than ever and has increased nutritional needs. Eating well during pregnancy is essential, not only for your own health but also for your baby’s growth and development.
Some of the key nutrients to focus on include protein, folate, iron, iodine, vitamin D and vitamin B6. These all play important roles in supporting your energy, immune system, and your baby’s healthy development.

#### Protein
Protein is essential during pregnancy as it supports the growth of your baby’s tissues, vital organs and brain. It also helps your own body by supporting the healthy growth of breast and uterine tissue and by contributing to the increase in blood supply needed during pregnancy.
Most pregnant women need around 75 to 100 grams of protein per day. This is about 2 to 3 servings of protein-rich foods. One serving is roughly the size of your palm (around 85 g).
Good sources of protein include fully cooked fish or seafood, chicken, lean beef, lamb, pork, tofu, legumes and nuts.

#### Folate
Folate is a B-group vitamin that plays a vital role in healthy growth and development, particularly in unborn babies. It is especially important in helping the nervous system form properly. Getting enough folate is critical in the months leading up to pregnancy and during the first trimester.
Folate is found naturally in green leafy vegetables such as spinach, broccoli and asparagus, as well as in fruits like strawberries, oranges and bananas, and in legumes such as chickpeas, beans and lentils.
Folic acid, the fortified form of folate, is added to some foods like cereals, cereal-based products and certain fruit juices (always check the label, as not all are fortified). In Australia, it is mandatory for all wheat flour used in bread-making to contain folic acid, so eating bread is often an easy way to boost your intake — unless you make your own or buy organic.
Because most women don’t get enough folate through food alone, a daily supplement of 500 mcg (0.5 mg) folic acid is recommended. Some women may require higher amounts, so it’s best to check with your dietitian or doctor to find what’s right for you.

#### Iron
Iron is essential during pregnancy because it helps your body make red blood cells, which carry oxygen to both you and your baby. Your developing baby also stores iron to last through the first five to six months after birth.
If you don’t get enough iron during pregnancy, there is a higher risk of complications such as pre-term delivery or low birth weight. That’s why it’s so important to include iron-rich foods in your diet every day.
The recommended daily intake of iron during pregnancy is 27 mg per day, which is about 9 mg more than for non-pregnant women. Even though you stop losing iron through menstruation during pregnancy, your baby’s needs make it necessary to boost your intake.
Iron from animal foods (such as lean red meat, chicken and fish) is absorbed easily by the body. Plant-based sources (such as beans, lentils and leafy greens) are not absorbed as well, but combining them with foods rich in vitamin C (like oranges, strawberries or tomatoes) can significantly improve absorption. This is especially important for vegetarian or vegan mums-to-be.

#### Iodine
Iodine is a key nutrient for your baby’s brain development. It helps your body produce thyroid hormones, which are essential for growth and development during pregnancy. If you don’t get enough iodine, it can increase your baby’s risk of developmental delays and thyroid problems.
Marine foods and iodised salt are good sources, but it’s important not to increase your salt intake just to boost iodine levels. In Australia, most bread-making flours — including those used for gluten-free breads — are now fortified with iodine (with the exception of organic and some yeast-free breads).
Other good food sources include seafood, seaweed, eggs, meat, dairy products and most commercially sold breads. Along with including iodine-rich foods in your diet, it is recommended that women planning a pregnancy, during pregnancy and while breastfeeding take a daily pregnancy multivitamin that contains 150 mcg of iodine.

#### Vitamin D
Vitamin D is important for both your health and your baby’s development during pregnancy. It helps your baby’s bones and teeth form properly, while also supporting your own muscle strength and overall wellbeing. Low levels of vitamin D in pregnancy can sometimes cause muscle weakness and pain.
The main source of vitamin D is sunlight. When your skin is exposed to ultraviolet (UV) rays from the sun, it naturally produces vitamin D. Smaller amounts can also be found in foods like oily fish, egg yolks, margarine and some brands of milk.
You may be more at risk of vitamin D deficiency if you:

- Have darker skin
- Cover most of your body with clothing
- Spend most of your time indoorsIf you fall into one of these groups, it’s a good idea to speak with your GP. They can arrange a blood test to check your vitamin D levels and recommend a supplement if needed during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

#### Calcium
Calcium plays an important role in pregnancy by helping to regulate fluids in your body and supporting the growth of your baby’s bones and teeth. During the third trimester, your baby’s need for calcium increases as their bones strengthen and develop. Amazingly, your body adapts by absorbing calcium more efficiently during pregnancy, which means your daily requirements don’t actually increase.
The recommended daily intake (RDI) for women remains the same during pregnancy and breastfeeding — 1,000 mg per day for women aged 19–50, and 1,300 mg per day for adolescents and women over 51.
To meet your calcium needs, include foods such as milk, cheese, yoghurt, or calcium-fortified soy milk in your diet. These are excellent and reliable sources to help you and your baby stay healthy.

#### Vitamin B6
During pregnancy, your blood volume increases, which is why your body needs more vitamin B6. Low levels of this vitamin have been linked with complications such as pre-eclampsia, high blood pressure, severe nausea and vomiting, and it may also play a role in your baby’s brain development.
The good news is that vitamin B6 is easy to get from a healthy, balanced diet. It is found in foods like fortified breakfast cereals, vegetables, fruits and meats — so most women can meet their needs without difficulty.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Proper nutrition is crucial during pregnancy because it provides the necessary nutrients to support the growth and development of the fetus, as well as maintain the health of the mother. Here are some reasons why proper nutrition is important during pregnancy:

● Fetal growth and development: The fetus requires a wide range of nutrients, such as protein, carbohydrates, healthy fats, vitamins, and minerals, for proper growth and development. Adequate nutrition during pregnancy helps ensure that the fetus receives these essential nutrients.

● Reduced risk of birth defects: Certain nutrients, such as folic acid, iron, and calcium, are particularly important during pregnancy for reducing the risk of birth defects.

● Maintaining maternal health: Pregnancy can put a strain on the mother’s body, and proper nutrition can help maintain the mother’s health and prevent complications such as anemia, gestational diabetes, and pre-eclampsia.

● Postnatal recovery: Adequate nutrition during pregnancy can also help with postnatal recovery by ensuring the mother has the necessary nutrients to heal and produce breast milk.

● Long-term health outcomes: Proper nutrition during pregnancy may also have long-term health outcomes for the child, such as reduced risk of chronic diseases such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension.

It’s important to work with a healthcare provider or registered dietitian to develop a personalised nutrition plan during pregnancy to ensure adequate nutrient intake for both the mother and the fetus.
