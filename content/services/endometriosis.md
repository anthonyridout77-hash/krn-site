Endometriosis affects many women of reproductive age, and for some it makes the path to pregnancy more challenging. For others it goes unnoticed until difficulties conceiving arise. Many women with endometriosis still conceive naturally and go on to have healthy pregnancies, and with the right medical care and personalised nutrition it is possible to manage symptoms and feel more in control of your health. ## What is endometriosis?

Endometriosis is a condition where tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside of it. This tissue can cause chronic inflammation and, in more advanced stages, scar tissue in the ovaries and endometriotic cysts. It affects around 6 to 10 per cent of women, and among women experiencing infertility, studies suggest 25 to 50 per cent may also have endometriosis. The exact cause is not known; possible factors include retrograde menstruation, hormonal imbalances, genetics, immune system issues and embryonic cell transformation.

Severity is measured in four stages:

- Stage I: a few small spots of endometrial tissue, no scar tissue. Some women have pelvic discomfort or cramping with their cycle; others have no symptoms.
- Stage II: more areas of tissue growth, still no scar tissue. Pelvic pain and cramps may become more noticeable.
- Stage III: a larger amount of tissue in the abdomen. Fluid-filled cysts (endometriomas) may develop on the ovaries and scar tissue may begin to form.
- Stage IV: the most advanced stage. Larger ovarian cysts, and scar tissue that can form between the uterus, ovaries, fallopian tubes and even the intestines.

## Common symptoms

Symptoms vary from woman to woman, and their severity does not always reflect the stage. Common symptoms include:

- Pelvic pain and cramping, often worse during menstrual cycles
- Pain during or after sexual activity
- Nausea, bloating or digestive upset, including diarrhoea or constipation around menstruation
- Painful bowel movements or urination, particularly during menstruation
- Heavy or irregular periods
- Fatigue and low energy
- A heavy or aching feeling in the legs, or lower back pain
- Abdominal, back, shoulder or chest pain that follows a cyclical pattern
- In some cases, difficulty conceiving

## Endometriosis and fertility

For some women, endometriosis makes it harder to conceive. This is often due to reduced egg quality, scarring of the fallopian tubes or ovaries, damage to the uterine lining, or changes within the pelvic organs caused by blocked tubes or scar tissue.

Even so, endometriosis-related infertility affects around one in four women with the condition, which means the majority go on to fall pregnant naturally. For some women with Stage I or II endometriosis, fertility may improve after surgery to remove endometrial tissue, although the benefit is often temporary. Depending on severity, treatments such as IVF or surgery may be needed to support conception.

## How nutrition can help

Because endometriosis is linked to chronic inflammation, often driven by digestive and immune imbalances, the right diet can help ease symptoms. Through targeted nutrition, Kat works with you to support your immune system and gut health and reduce the inflammation that drives endometriosis. If you are planning a pregnancy, she can build a personalised plan that supports your fertility and gives you a solid foundation for pregnancy.

Dietary approaches that may help include:

- Anti-inflammatory foods: fruits, vegetables, whole grains and healthy fats from fish, nuts and seeds
- Omega-3 fats from oily fish such as salmon and sardines, walnuts, flaxseeds and chia seeds
- Fibre-rich foods such as fruit, vegetables, whole grains and legumes to support digestion and regular bowel movements
- Anti-inflammatory spices such as turmeric, ginger and cinnamon, and lean proteins such as chicken, fish and tofu
- Cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts), which contain compounds that support oestrogen metabolism
- Staying well hydrated, which aids digestion and helps prevent constipation

Some people find certain foods worsen their symptoms. Common triggers include red and processed meats, high-fat dairy, gluten-containing foods, soy products, alcohol, caffeine, sugar and high-GI foods, and fried or high-fat foods. Triggers vary from person to person, so a food diary or a supervised elimination approach can help identify yours. No diet treats endometriosis on its own, and dietary changes belong alongside medical care.

## How is endometriosis diagnosed?

Diagnosis usually combines your medical history and symptoms, a physical examination, and sometimes imaging such as ultrasound or MRI, which can show larger cysts and adhesions but may miss smaller lesions. Laparoscopy, a minimally invasive surgical procedure using a small camera, is the gold standard, and a biopsy taken during laparoscopy confirms the diagnosis. If you suspect you have endometriosis, see your GP, who can guide you through the process and treatment options such as pain medication, hormone therapy and surgery.
