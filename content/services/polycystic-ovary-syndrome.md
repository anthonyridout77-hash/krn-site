Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common hormonal conditions affecting women of reproductive age, and it can feel overwhelming when you are trying to conceive. Many women worry that PCOS means they will not be able to fall pregnant, but there is plenty of hope. With the right care, lifestyle changes and nutrition support, pregnancy is still very possible. This page is part of Kat's [pre-conception nutrition](/our-services/prenatal-nutrition-and-dietetics/) support.

## Pregnancy is possible with PCOS

While PCOS can affect hormone balance and make ovulation less predictable, it does not mean pregnancy is out of reach. Many women with PCOS conceive and go on to have healthy pregnancies. Personalised nutrition, alongside medical care, can help balance hormones and support ovulation.

## What are the symptoms?

PCOS looks different for every woman, but common symptoms include:

- Irregular or absent menstrual periods
- Difficulty ovulating, which may affect fertility
- Unexplained weight changes or difficulty losing weight
- Excess hair growth on the face or body
- Thinning hair on the scalp
- Acne or oily skin
- Darkened patches of skin, often around the neck or underarms

Because PCOS can interfere with ovulation, it may make natural conception more challenging and, in some cases, increase the risk of miscarriage. Younger women often notice more pronounced symptoms. Some symptoms, like irregular ovulation, can become less pronounced with age, but overall fertility still declines naturally over time, so getting support earlier is worthwhile.

## How PCOS can affect pregnancy

PCOS can increase certain risks during pregnancy, including miscarriage, gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, macrosomia (a larger than average baby) and a higher chance of your baby needing care in a neonatal intensive care unit after birth.

Steps that help lower these risks include:

- Reaching and maintaining a healthy weight before pregnancy
- Supporting balanced blood sugar levels before and during pregnancy
- Taking the right folic acid and prenatal supplements, as recommended by your healthcare provider

## Treatment options to help you conceive

- Lifestyle and weight management: for women above a healthy weight, even modest weight loss through regular movement and a balanced, targeted diet can help restore hormonal balance and regulate cycles.
- Medication: your doctor may prescribe medication to support ovulation. It is most helpful combined with healthy lifestyle changes and a structured nutrition plan.
- IVF: in some cases IVF may be recommended. An egg is fertilised with sperm in a laboratory and the embryo transferred into the uterus. See [IVF nutrition](/our-services/prenatal-nutrition-and-dietetics/ivf-nutrition/).
- Surgery: if other options are not successful, a procedure called ovarian drilling may be considered, where small openings are made in the ovary's surface to restore ovulation. This is usually a short-term option, lasting around six to eight months.

Whichever path is right for you, nutrition plays a part in supporting your fertility and pregnancy.

## How nutrition can help

Because PCOS is closely linked to insulin resistance and inflammation, the right foods can help regulate blood sugar, reduce inflammation and support more regular cycles. Through personalised nutrition, Kat helps you create a plan that fits your lifestyle and eases your symptoms, focusing on small, realistic changes.

Diet and lifestyle changes can help with:

- Insulin sensitivity: balanced meals and regular movement help regulate blood sugar
- Inflammation: anti-inflammatory foods such as fruit, vegetables, nuts, seeds and healthy fats
- Weight management, especially for symptoms connected to insulin resistance
- More regular menstrual cycles
- Fertility, by supporting ovulation and egg quality
- Long-term heart health, since women with PCOS have a higher risk of cardiovascular disease

## What does a PCOS-friendly diet look like?

Include plenty of:

- Complex carbohydrates such as whole grains, oats, quinoa and brown rice
- Lean proteins such as chicken, fish, eggs, legumes and tofu
- Healthy fats from avocados, nuts, seeds and olive oil
- Low-GI foods to help stabilise blood sugar
- Fibre-rich foods including leafy greens, colourful vegetables and fresh fruit
- Omega-3 fats from salmon, sardines, chia seeds or flaxseeds

Limit or avoid processed foods and sugary snacks or drinks, high-fat dairy, foods high in saturated and trans fats, excessive caffeine and alcohol. The same principles apply during pregnancy with PCOS, where limiting refined carbohydrates and added sugars is also important for blood sugar control. If gestational diabetes develops, see [Gestational Diabetes Mellitus](/our-services/pregnancy-nutrition-and-dietetics/gestational-diabetes/).

## Do supplements help with PCOS and fertility?

Certain supplements may support women with PCOS who are trying to conceive, including myo-inositol, folic acid, vitamin D, omega-3 fats and magnesium, and in some cases zinc, chromium or probiotics. Supplements should complement a balanced diet, not replace it, and the right choice depends on your situation, so check with your dietitian or doctor before starting anything.
