## With the right guidance, PCOS doesn’t have to stand in the way of your dream of motherhood

Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common hormonal conditions affecting women of reproductive age, and it can feel overwhelming when you are trying to conceive. Many women worry that PCOS means they won’t be able to fall pregnant, but the truth is there is plenty of hope. With the right care, lifestyle changes and nutrition support, pregnancy is still very possible.

### Pregnancy is possible with PCOS
Many women with PCOS are able to conceive and go on to have healthy pregnancies. While PCOS can affect hormone balance and make ovulation less predictable, it does not mean that pregnancy is out of reach.
The encouraging news is that PCOS can be managed, and with the right support, many women successfully fall pregnant. Nutrition can help with insulin resistance, hunger and cravings, and regular eating, alongside any medical treatment from your GP or specialist. A weight-neutral approach is always available.

### How does PCOS affect pregnancy?
PCOS can increase certain risks during pregnancy, including:

- Miscarriage
- Gestational diabetes
- Preeclampsia
- Macrosomia (a larger than average baby)
- A higher chance of your baby needing care in a neonatal intensive unit after birth
### How can I reduce risks from PCOS during pregnancy?
The good news is that there are many ways to lower these risks and support a healthy pregnancy. Steps you can take include:

- Reaching and maintaining a healthy weight before pregnancy
- Supporting balanced blood sugar levels before and during pregnancy
- Taking the right folic acid and prenatal supplements
### What are my treatment options for PCOS to help me fall pregnant?
If you have PCOS, there are several options available to help you conceive. With the right medical care and personalised nutrition support, many women with PCOS are able to fall pregnant and carry a healthy pregnancy.

#### Lifestyle and weight management
For women who are above a healthy weight, even modest weight loss through regular movement and a balanced, targeted diet can make cycles more regular for some people. Weight change is offered, never assumed.

#### Medication
Your doctor may prescribe medication to support ovulation. While medicine can be helpful, it works best when combined with healthy lifestyle changes and a structured nutrition plan.

#### IVF (In vitro fertilisation)
In some cases, IVF may be recommended. This involves fertilising an egg with sperm in a laboratory, then transferring the embryo into the uterus to support pregnancy.

#### Surgery
If other options are not successful, surgery may be considered. In women with PCOS, the outer shell of the ovaries can sometimes prevent ovulation. A procedure called ovarian drilling may be used, where small openings are made in the ovary’s surface to restore ovulation. This is usually a short-term option, lasting around six to eight months.
No matter which treatment path is right for you, nutrition plays a vital role in supporting your fertility and pregnancy outcomes. ### What are the symptoms of Polycystic Ovary Syndrome?
PCOS can look different for every woman, but some of the more common symptoms include:

- Irregular or absent menstrual periods
- Difficulty ovulating, which may affect fertility
- Unexplained weight changes or difficulty losing weight
- Excess hair growth on the face or body
- Thinning hair on the scalp
- Acne or oily skin
- Darkened patches of skin (often around the neck or underarms)Because PCOS can interfere with ovulation, it may make natural conception more challenging and, in some cases, increase the risk of miscarriage. This is why seeking medical, health and dietary support is so important.
Younger women often experience more noticeable symptoms due to higher hormone levels and a greater degree of imbalance. While some PCOS symptoms, like irregular ovulation, can sometimes become less pronounced as women age, overall female fertility still naturally declines with age — so working with a dietitian earlier can help optimise your chances.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can changes to your diet and lifestyle help PCOS?

Making changes to your diet and lifestyle can play a big role in managing Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) and improving overall health. Some of the key benefits include:

- Improving insulin sensitivity: PCOS is often linked with insulin resistance. A balanced diet and regular movement can help regulate blood sugar levels.

- Reducing inflammation: Eating more anti-inflammatory foods like fruits, vegetables, nuts, seeds and healthy fats can help ease PCOS-related inflammation.

- Supporting weight management: Reaching and maintaining a healthy weight can improve PCOS symptoms, especially those connected to insulin resistance.

- Regulating menstrual cycles: Healthy eating patterns and weight management can help restore more regular cycles.

- Preparing for pregnancy: regular meals, enough protein and fibre, and the right preconception supplements, alongside any medical fertility treatment.

- Reducing long-term risks: Women with PCOS have a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, but good nutrition and exercise can help protect heart health.

It’s important to make these changes alongside medical advice. Working with a fertility dietitian like Kat Ridout ensures that your nutrition plan is personalised, evidence-based and designed to support both your symptoms and your fertility goals.

### What should we include or avoid in a PCOS-friendly diet?

A PCOS-friendly diet focuses on regular meals, carbohydrate quality and spread, and protein and fibre at each meal.

Include plenty of:

- Complex carbohydrates like whole grains, oats, quinoa and brown rice

- Lean proteins such as chicken, fish, eggs, legumes and tofu

- Healthy fats from avocados, nuts, seeds and olive oil

- Low-glycemic index foods to help stabilise blood sugar

- Fibre-rich foods including leafy greens, colourful vegetables and fresh fruit

Limit or avoid:

- Processed foods and sugary snacks or drinks

- High-fat dairy products

- Foods high in saturated and trans fats

- Excessive caffeine

- Alcohol

Every woman with PCOS has unique needs, so it’s best to work with a fertility dietitian like Kat Ridout to create a personalised nutrition plan that supports your symptoms, fertility and overall wellbeing.

### What should I eat during pregnancy to manage PCOS symptoms?

Managing PCOS during pregnancy is possible with a balanced, nutrient-rich diet that supports both your health and your baby’s development. Focus on:

- Low-GI foods such as whole grains, legumes and non-starchy vegetables to stabilise blood sugar and improve insulin sensitivity

- Lean proteins like chicken, fish, eggs, tofu and legumes

- Healthy fats from avocado, olive oil, nuts and seeds to reduce inflammation and nourish your body

- High-fibre foods including leafy greens, berries and whole grains to aid digestion and support healthy weight management

- Omega-3 fatty acids from salmon, sardines, chia seeds or flaxseeds to help reduce inflammation and support overall health

- Hydration and supplements such as folic acid, as recommended by your healthcare provider, to support foetal growth and development

Limiting processed sugars and refined carbohydrates is also important, as they can worsen insulin resistance and make PCOS symptoms harder to manage.

### How can I manage gestational diabetes during pregnancy if I have PCOS?

To manage gestational diabetes during pregnancy with PCOS in Queensland, focus on a low-GI diet with whole grains, non-starchy vegetables, and lean proteins to regulate blood sugar. Eating smaller, frequent meals and including healthy fats like avocado and nuts will support balanced blood sugar levels. Regular exercise can improve insulin sensitivity but always check with your doctor before starting any new activities. Monitoring your blood sugar regularly and staying hydrated with water, avoiding sugary drinks, are essential.
